### How to make an interviewer think you re capable

## Core principles

- **Capability in interviews is signaled, not proven.** Years of work are invisible in 30 minutes; you must deliberately show three signals: how fast you think, how good your thinking is, and how clearly you say it.
- **None of these three signals are your raw skills list.** Technical ability alone does not make interviewers think you're capable.
- **Preparation beats improvisation.** High-stakes conversations (in-laws, big meetings) succeed because you prepare opinions in advance — interviews are the same.
- **Filler words are a thinking problem, not a speaking problem.** Silence is better than "um / maybe / I think" when your thought isn't ready.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan evaluates candidates as an interviewer with limited time (~30 minutes). The hiring manager listens **top-down** (why it mattered → how you solved it), while most candidates narrate **bottom-up** (what I did → why → impact). Chinese candidates especially excel at zooming in but forget to zoom out to business impact.

Interviewers have **zero context** on you. In internal meetings, slack is given; in interviews, every hesitant phrase becomes a negative data point about confidence.

## Evaluation criteria

Score whether the candidate demonstrates:

1. **Thinking speed (prepared, not on-the-spot)** — Do they have pre-formed opinions on the hiring manager's pain, their unique offer, and their obvious weakness?
2. **Thinking quality (zoom in + zoom out)** — Can they explain *why the business cared* before diving into tasks?
3. **Communication clarity** — Confident delivery without filler; silence instead of uncertainty language.

Red flags: describing work without business impact; scrambling on predictable questions; "I think maybe we could…" language.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Listing skills and project details without showing the three signals.
- Answering stakeholder / cross-functional questions by scrambling instead of returning to a pre-formed opinion.
- Describing achievements bottom-up (tasks first) when hiring managers want impact first.
- Over-indexing on removing filler words mechanically instead of preparing thoughts so filler isn't needed.
- Forgetting to prepare for the **most obvious reason they wouldn't hire you** (gap, missing skill, etc.).

## Follow-up questions

When answers lack capability signals, ask:

- "What is the hiring manager's biggest pain point for this role — and how does your story address it?"
- "Why did this matter to the business? What would have broken if you hadn't done this?"
- "What's the most obvious reason we might not hire you — and what's your prepared response?"
- "Can you flip this: start with business impact, then your solution?"
- "Where are you filling silence with uncertainty language instead of pausing?"

## Answer frameworks

**Before the interview — prepare three opinions:**

1. Hiring manager's biggest pain (read JD; e.g. "cross-functional collaboration" on a technical role = not a code monkey).
2. What you specifically offer that addresses that pain (stories + proof).
3. The most obvious reason they wouldn't hire you (intelligent, persuasive answer ready).

**During answers — impact-first template:**

> The business impact from this project was X, the key problems I addressed are Y, and I solved it by doing Z.

**Communication:** Replace filler with silence; prepare thoughts so you don't need "um / maybe."

## Good vs bad examples

### High-stakes prep: you already know how to do this

**Scenario:** Candidate thinks they must "think fast on the spot" in interviews.

**Jonathan's analogy (verbatim):**

> I remember when I met my in-laws for the first time. I sat my wife down for 10 minutes, and I interrogated her. What kind of guy do they like? What did they dislike in your previous boyfriends?

> See, you already know how to prepare for high-stakes conversations, so do the same thing for the interview.

**Bad:** Treating the interview as improvisation — trying to invent opinions when the question lands.

**Good:** Before you walk in, develop a clear point of view on three things (his list): hiring manager's biggest pain, what you offer that addresses it, and the most obvious reason they wouldn't hire you.

---

### Reading the JD: cross-functional collaboration on a technical role

**Scenario:** Job description lists collaboration skills for a technical role.

**Jonathan's read (near-verbatim):**

> If they list strong cross-functional collaboration for a technical role, that is a sign that they are not looking for a code monkey. They really value your ability to work with stakeholders.

**Bad:** Preparing only technical stories; scrambling when a behavioral question appears.

**Good:** Form one clear opinion on what good stakeholder management looks like — then reuse it across questions.

---

### Same prepared opinion, two different questions (no scrambling)

**Scenario:** Interviewer asks either of these:

1. > "How do you handle a stakeholder who resists change?"
2. > "Tell me about a time when you worked cross-functionally."

**Bad:** Scrambling for a new answer each time; disconnected stories.

**Good (Jonathan's move):**

> Bam, you go back to the opinion that you formed about what good stakeholder management looks like.

> Or if they ask, tell me about a time when you worked cross-functionally. You go back to that same opinion.

> No more scrambling, now you're ready.

---

### Third opinion: the reason they might not hire you

**Scenario:** Obvious gap — career break, missing skill, etc.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> What is the most obvious reason that they wouldn't hire you? Yeah, you do need this opinion because they will dig into it. Whether it's a career gap or a missing skill, whatever, you have something intelligent and persuasive ready when they ask you about it.

**Bad:** Hoping they won't ask; vague or defensive when they do.

**Good:** A pre-formed, intelligent, persuasive answer — ready before the interview.

---

### Thinking quality: bottom-up vs top-down

**Scenario:** Describing a project or achievement.

**Bad (how most candidates narrate — Jonathan's words):**

> Most people describe their work from the bottom up. Here's what I did, here's why I did it, here's the impact.

**Good (how hiring managers listen — Jonathan's words):**

> But hiring managers listen from the top down. They want to know why it mattered first, so flip it. Start with the business impact, then your solution, like this template.

**Jonathan's template (keep placeholders):**

> The business impact from this project was X, the key problems I addressed are Y, and I solved it by doing Z.

**Zoom-out questions he tells candidates to ask themselves:**

> Why does this matter to the business? How did this project actually help the business to succeed? Or what would have broken if I hadn't done this?

---

### Filler words: thinking problem, not speaking problem

**Scenario:** Candidate says they can't stop saying um, like, maybe.

**Jonathan on the common comment (verbatim):**

> How do I remove my filler words? I say, um, like, maybe, so much, and I can't stop.

**His reframe:**

> Filler words are not a speaking problem. They are a thinking problem. You're filling the silence because your thought isn't ready yet. And in an interview, uncertain language is deadly.

**Bad — team meeting vs interview (his contrast):**

> In a meeting with your own team, people already know you, right? They'll cut you some slack. But in an interview, your interviewer has zero context on you. Every, oh, I think maybe, um, uh, that is only a data point that tells them this person is not confident.

**Good:**

> So instead of filling your sentences with all these filler words, fill them with silence.

**Bad (candidate language):** > "Oh, I think maybe, um, uh…"

**Good (candidate behavior):** Silence until the thought is ready — then a clear, confident point.

### What s your greatest weakness The only answer that works

## Core principles

- **Weakness questions are really about personal growth** — not trick questions to eliminate you.
- **The right answer shows three signals:** self-awareness, confidence, and bias to action.
- **Honesty beats polish.** Jonathan (as hiring manager) trusts answers that are "messy and ugly and human" more than rehearsed corporate weaknesses.
- **A good weakness answer helps your future manager** — it gives them information to support you (e.g. how to deliver feedback constructively).

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan has **never asked this question as a hiring manager** because he doesn't like it — but when interviewers do ask, they're listening for whether you can recognize gaps, believe you can change, and have already taken action.

The best answers make the interviewer **trust you**, not impress them with a fake "I work too hard" weakness.

## Evaluation criteria

Score the answer on:

1. **Self-awareness** — Do they name a real, non-critical growth area and understand its impact?
2. **Confidence** — Do they believe they can change (not helpless, not defensive)?
3. **Bias to action** — Concrete steps already taken + measurable improvement?

**Trust signal (Jonathan's bar):** Would you hold this weakness against them? Does honesty make you want to nurture a better working relationship?

Red flags: fake strengths disguised as weaknesses; no action taken; weakness that disqualifies them for the role.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Treating it as a trap instead of a growth story.
- Sharing something too safe / generic ("I'm a perfectionist") that interviewers can't relate to.
- Sharing a **critical** weakness that should disqualify you for the role.
- No concrete improvement steps — only acknowledging the flaw.
- Being too polished — sounds scripted, not human.

## Follow-up questions

When answers are weak, ask:

- "What specific steps have you taken — not plan to take — to improve?"
- "How has that weakness shown up in your last role? Give me one example."
- "What improvement can you point to so far?"
- "Is this weakness relevant to the role you're applying for? How do you manage it on the job?"

## Answer frameworks

**Jonathan's formula (verbatim structure):**

> My weakness is, [real non-critical growth area, and I know it can have whatever impact on your career]. I've taken steps to improve by [concrete specific steps], and so far, I've seen [this improvement].

**Three pillars to weave in:**

1. Self-awareness — name the gap and its real impact on you / work
2. Confidence — reflection on root cause + belief you can change
3. Bias to action — specific practices + results

## Good vs bad examples

### What interviewers are actually testing

**Scenario:** "What's your greatest weakness?"

**Jonathan's reframe:**

> Weakness questions are actually questions about personal growth. And to grow, you need these three things, right? You need self-awareness, that these are gaps and you need to close them. Second, you need the self-confidence to believe that you can change, and third, you need to actually take action to change.

**Bad:** "I'm a perfectionist" / "I work too hard" with no growth story.

**Good:** Real growth area + vulnerability + steps taken + improvement shown.

---

### Why honesty wins (hiring manager reaction)

**Jonathan on his favorite coached answer:**

> What I personally love, love, love about this answer from my hiring manager perspective is that I trust her, because this answer is messy and ugly and human, one that I can relate to as well, and one that I wouldn't hold against her. Knowing this as her future manager would actually help me to plan how I deliver feedback constructively, and I'd like to think that honesty would actually nurture a better relationship.

**Bad:** An answer so polished you suspect it's performance.

**Good:** Human, specific, helps the manager understand how to work with you.

---

### Answers most people are too afraid to give

**Jonathan's setup:**

> It's an answer that I think most people would be too afraid to share. It was actually from an associate director of analytics, and she told me…

**Bad:** Choosing a weakness you think is "safe" but sounds inauthentic.

**Good:** A real non-critical growth area you're willing to own in front of a stranger.

## Jonathan's sample answers (verbatim)

### Coached answer — associate director of analytics (full script)

- **Central question this Short addresses:** What's your greatest weakness?
- **Interview question(s) this sample answers:** "What's your greatest weakness?" / weakness / areas for development
- **Jonathan's sample answer (verbatim):**

> My greatest weakness is that I'm too hard on myself when others give me feedback. If I receive criticism, I really take it personally, and I have a hard time getting over it. I've discussed this with people close to me, and after some reflection, I think it comes from my lack of confidence from within. I hate to think that I'm not useful to others. This pattern has really drained my energy, and I know that to change, I need to be the one who believes in myself the most. So, I've been practicing by documenting my achievements. When I notice that I'm struggling with criticism from others, I go back to these achievements, reminding myself of my impact. By striking this better balance, I've spent much less time agonizing over feedback and more time delivering results.

## Core principles

- **You can break into any industry or job title with the right strategy** — Jonathan's corporate → tech pivot used three steps.
- **Change one dimension at a time** — either job title *or* industry, so you still show clear relevance.
- **Study who already fits** — networking, coffee chats, LinkedIn pattern-spotting; then cover your gaps with demonstrated skills.
- **You can't invent missing experience — but you can fill gaps with stories** that make interviewers see a "superstar who achieves great things wherever they go."
- **Interviews are storytelling contests, not status updates** — stop STAR (reporting format); use HEALER (outlined elsewhere in his content; letters not expanded in this Short).

## Interview philosophy

Hiring managers take chances when relevance is clear. Same-level pivots (e.g. senior DS → senior AI PM in banking) are possible if stories prove the adjacent skills even without the title. Quantity of prepared BQ stories is the wrong game — **three highest-impact "superstar" stories** beat one story per question.

## Evaluation criteria

Score transition candidates on:

1. **Relevance strategy** — Only one of title/industry changed at a time
2. **Market research** — Knows what the ideal profile looks like and which gaps to cover
3. **Resume storytelling** — Problem → skills → achievement (not bare responsibilities or random numbers)
4. **Interview storytelling** — Few deep impact stories with layered proof of target-role skills
5. **Format** — Storytelling over STAR-style reporting

## Common candidate mistakes

- Trying to change job title **and** industry at once in a competitive market.
- Resume bullets that are only responsibilities, or "achievements" that are random numbers without a story.
- Preparing one unique story for every behavioral question (exhausting, doesn't stand out).
- Using STAR as if the interview were a status update.
- Assuming you must start at the bottom for any title change (same-level pivots can work with the right stories).

## Follow-up questions

When pivot answers are weak, ask:

- "Are you changing title, industry, or both — and where's the clear relevance?"
- "What patterns did you see in LinkedIn profiles of people already in that role?"
- "What's the challenge, the skill you used, and the business impact — in one bullet?"
- "What are your three proudest, highest-impact stories — and which target-role skills do they prove?"

## Answer frameworks

**Three steps to break in:**
1. Change **one** thing: title *or* industry (Jonathan: strategy & ops titles → same titles in tech).
2. Learn who fits: events, coffee chats, LinkedIn creep; note shared traits; cover gaps (e.g. consulting background → show strategic thinking).
3. Look like the ideal candidate via **stories** on resume + interview.

**Resume bullet format:** Problem → Skills → Achievement  
Definition of a story: challenge faced + relevant skills used + how it helped team/business.

**Interview:** Three superstar stories (proudest, highest impact); layer in details that prove target skills without the title.

**Anti-pattern:** STAR = reporting format. Prefer HEALER (framework named here; letter breakdown not in this transcript).

## Good vs bad examples

### Step 1 — one change at a time

**Scenario:** Pivoting careers.

**Jonathan's rule (verbatim):**

> Step one is to pick one thing to change at a time, either your job title or your industry. Because in a competitive market, if you want an interviewer to take a chance on you, you have to show clear relevance first.

**His move (verbatim):**

> So my job title at the time was in strategy and operations, and I applied to the same types of titles, but in the tech industry.

**Bad:** Applying for a new title in a new industry with no bridging relevance.

**Good:** Same title family, new industry (or same industry, adjacent title).

---

### Step 2 — study the tribe

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Step two is to learn what kinds of people fit in that role or industry. Go to their networking events, have coffee chats with them. For my own transition, I even creeped LinkedIn profiles of people who worked in my target companies. And I observed the characteristics they shared in common.

> Like, I realized that many of them came from management consulting backgrounds. I didn't, so I knew that I would have to show my strategic thinking skills to cover that gap.

---

### Resume: responsibilities vs story

**Bad (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> Most people will write responsibilities, and some might add some random numbers and call it an achievement. But neither of these things is a story.

**Good definition (verbatim):**

> A story is what challenge you faced, what relevant skills you used, and how it helped your team or business.

**Format (verbatim):**

> Problem, skills, achievement.

**Bad:** One story per behavioral question — "going crazy."

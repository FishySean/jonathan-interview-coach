## Core principles

- **Three kinds of interview questions — each needs a completely different answer.**
- **Only behavioral questions get full stories** (STAR / CAR / PAR / HEALER).
- **Situational = future/hypothetical** — direct answer, not a long STAR dump.
- **Common questions** (tell me about yourself, why here) — carefully scripted, tailored; again **no stories**.
- Mixing types is a **simple, costly mistake** — common among Chinese candidates (and even some coaches); it signals you don’t understand how Western interviews work.

## Interview philosophy

Interviewers encode the expected answer shape in the question wording. Giving a behavioral story when they asked “how would you…” makes them lose patience or zone out and brand you a bad communicator — even if your content was “great.”

## Evaluation criteria

| Type | How to spot | What they want |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Behavioral** | Past-focused; “tell me about a time when…” / “give me an example of…” | Story via STAR/CAR/PAR/HEALER |
| **Situational** | Future/hypothetical; “how would you…” / “what would you do if…” | Direct knowledge/skill answer; optional 2-sentence mini-proof |
| **Common** | Recurring classics; tell me about yourself, why this company | Scripted, role/company-tailored; no stories |

Situational formula signal: **Most people do X, I do Y, because Z** — shows differentiation and summarized experience.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Using STAR on a non-behavioral question (viewer asked; Jonathan: “It wasn’t a behavioral question”).
- Treating “How do you prioritize work?” as BQ and dumping full S-T-A-R.
- Confusing situational or common with behavioral — especially costly for Chinese candidates in Western markets.
- Coaches/experts mixing the types too.

## Follow-up questions

- “Is this past (‘tell me about a time’) or future (‘how would you’)?”
- “Did they ask for a story — or a direct approach?”
- “For situational: what’s my Most people X / I do Y / because Z?”
- “For common: is my script tailored — and story-free?”

## Answer frameworks

1. **Behavioral** — STAR, CAR, PAR, or HEALER (memorable stories).
2. **Situational** — `Most people do X, I do Y, because Z` + optional ≤2-sentence mini story.
3. **Common** — carefully scripted, tailored to role/company; no stories.

## Good vs bad examples

### Mislabeling a question as behavioral

**Scenario:** Viewer: for this behavioral question, shouldn’t we use STAR?

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> It wasn't a behavioral question… Not just from candidates, but from other coaches and even career experts as well, everyone's mixing up these questions.

---

### Situational treated as BQ

**Scenario:** “How do you prioritize work?”

**Bad (verbatim):**

> yeah, let me tell you about this huge project I worked on. This was the situation, my task, my actions, and my results. Your interviewer is going to lose their patience and interrupt you, or they're just going to zone out and think you're a bad communicator, because they didn't ask for a long story.

**Good formula (verbatim):**

> Most people do X, I do Y, because Z.

**Good example (verbatim):**

> most people prioritize by asking their boss. But my approach is to compare the urgency and importance of my tasks to form a hypothesis. I do this because I believe in doing the mental work upfront to make decision making easier for my leaders.

**Mini-proof rule (verbatim):**

> To prove your experience, you can always add a short mini story here, but it has to be concise, like two sentences max.

---

### Cost of the mix-up

**Why it matters (verbatim):**

> Many Chinese candidates confuse situational or common questions with behavioral questions. And this mistake doesn't just confuse the interviewer. It signals that you don't understand how interviews work in the West. You thought that you were giving a great answer. They thought you didn't get it. And now you know why.

## Core principles

- **Buzzwords ≠ approach.** Saying "common ground / active listening / compromise" without proving what you said, what the common ground was, and relationship impact fails the follow-up.
- **Two ways to treat conflict:** (1) battle — one wins, one loses; (2) information — opportunity to understand how the other person thinks and strengthen the working relationship. The second is the expected one.
- **Conflict as a gift.** When someone pushes back, assume there's a reason you can't see yet; listen until you see it *and* they feel fully heard; then look for shared goals.
- **Prove it, don't recite it.** Interviewers score whether you have an *effective approach*, not whether you can talk about conflict.
- **Abstract upward to find shared goals.** If timeline / project / team goals don't align, keep going higher — there's always a shared goal if you look high enough (including helping the company make money).

## Interview philosophy

Framed as a hiring manager giving honest rejection feedback: the candidate sounded polished (even AI-like) but blanked on specifics. Roles that require saying no to stakeholders need people who treat conflict as information/gift — like the prior hire whom "even the toughest stakeholders loved." Generic soft-skill language without proof reads as "you just knew how to talk about it."

## Evaluation criteria

Score whether the candidate:

1. **Has a real approach** — Not just buzzwords; can answer follow-ups with specifics
2. **Treats conflict as information / gift** — Not win/lose battle framing
3. **Can name the common ground** — What was shared, what they actually said
4. **Shows relationship impact** — How the conflict affected the long-term working relationship
5. **Can abstract to shared goals** — Ladder from timeline → project → team → company when agreement isn't obvious

Red flags: blanking on "what did you actually say?"; sounding like an AI template; treating conflict as something to "win."

## Common candidate mistakes

- Answering with generic phrases: find common ground, active listening, compromise — then blanking on follow-ups.
- Confusing *saying* shared goals with *proving* how you found them.
- Treating conflict as a battle (win/lose) instead of information.
- Not being able to climb the abstraction ladder when common goals aren't obvious.
- Over-relying on AI / buzzword answers that collapse under "what did you actually say?"

## Follow-up questions

Jonathan's own probe (use when answers are generic):

- "What did you actually say to find common ground?"
- "What was the common ground?"
- "How did that impact your long-term relationship?"

Coach probes:

- "When someone pushed back, what reason couldn't you see yet — and how did you listen until you saw it?"
- "Walk me up the ladder: timeline → project → team → company — where did you find the shared goal?"
- "Did you treat that conflict as a battle or as information?"

## Answer frameworks

**Drop buzzwords — two frames:**
1. Battle: one wins, one loses
2. Information: understand how they think + strengthen the relationship (preferred)

**Conflict-as-gift sequence (from prior high performer, via Jonathan):**
1. Someone pushes back → assume there's a reason you can't see
2. Listen until *you* can see it **and** they feel fully heard
3. Then start looking for shared goals

**Shared-goal abstraction ladder (when common goals aren't obvious):**
1. Can't agree on timeline → try broader **project** goal
2. Can't agree on that → try **team's** overall goal
3. Still stuck → same company: shared goal that you're trying to help the company make money
4. Rule: there's always a shared goal if you're willing to look high enough

## Good vs bad examples

### Rejection feedback: polished talk vs blank follow-up

**Scenario:** Candidate rejected after conflict question.

**Candidate's original answer (verbatim, as they recount it):**

> Of course, I said I try to find common ground, make everyone feel heard through active listening, and find a compromise.

**Hiring manager follow-up (verbatim):**

> Right, and then I asked you what did you actually say to find common ground? What was the common ground, and how did that impact your long-term relationship?

**Candidate reaction (verbatim):**

> Yeah, that follow-up was rough. I kind of blanked.

**Verdict (verbatim):**

> That was a moment I felt you didn't actually have an effective approach to conflict. You just knew how to talk about it.

---

### Battle vs information

**Scenario:** How people approach conflict.

**Bad frame (verbatim):**

> One way is to treat conflict like a battle. One of us has to win, the other has to lose, right?

**Good frame (verbatim):**

> The other way is treating conflict as information, an opportunity to understand how the other person thinks, and strengthen the working relationship.

**Hiring manager on whether the second is better (verbatim):**

> It concerns me that you even have to ask.

---

### Prior hire: conflict as a gift

**Scenario:** Role requires saying no to stakeholders; previous person thrived.

**Setup (verbatim):**

> The last person in this role was awesome at navigating these conflicts. This role involved saying no to stakeholders all the time. But even the toughest stakeholders loved her. I remember once I asked her if she needed me to support her in a meeting with a director who was mad at her decision. And she was like, no, I'm actually low-key looking forward to it. I treat conflict as a gift.

**Her method (verbatim):**

> Yeah, she was like, when someone pushes back, I assume there's a reason I can't see it. I listen until I can see it, and until they feel fully heard. And then I start looking for shared goals.

**Why candidate still failed (verbatim):**

> Yeah, but the problem isn't what you said. You didn't prove it.

---

### Finding common goals when they aren't obvious

**Scenario:** How the prior hire actually found shared goals.

**Method (verbatim):**

> When the common goals aren't obvious, you keep abstracting them. If we can't agree on a timeline, can we agree on the broader project goal? If we can't agree on that, can we agree on our team's overall goal? We all work at the same company. At the end of the day, one goal that we share is that we're trying to help the company make money, right? There's always a shared goal if you're willing to look high enough.

At the end of the day, one goal that we share is that we're trying to help the company make money, right? There's always a shared goal if you're willing to look high enough.

## Core principles

- **"Learning more" is often disguised avoidance.** When you feel behind (AI, layoffs, market shifts), the instinct is to take another course — but that can be a way to stay busy without risking the pain of rejection.
- **Applying is how you discover your real skill-market fit** — not studying. You cannot know which of your perceived gaps are real gaps until you test yourself against the actual market.
- **Build your spikes, don't close every gap.** Trying to become equally strong everywhere wastes time you could spend doubling down on what already makes you uniquely competitive.
- **Even failure has value.** Applying and getting rejected still teaches you your superpower, what the market actually wants, and gives you a game plan if the worst happens.

## Interview philosophy

This Short is about the mindset *before* you get to the interview stage, not about how interviewers score you. Jonathan's implicit view: interviewers/hiring processes reveal signal you can't get from self-study — e.g. he discovered through real interviews that "nobody in the hiring process could tell stories the way that I could," and that he "crushed every single case interview in preparation, like 100% success rate." The market — not your own assumptions — tells you where your real strengths and weaknesses are.

## Evaluation criteria

Not a scoring rubric in this Short. The closest equivalent: the real market (interviews, case rounds) is the only valid test of whether a skill is a genuine strength or gap — self-assessment based on comparing yourself to others (e.g. "my wife codes fluently, no sweat") is unreliable.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Treating courses/certifications as a substitute for actually applying.
- Staying busy so you can avoid the risk of being proven "not good enough" by rejection — hiding disguised as preparation.
- Trying to close every skill gap instead of identifying and leaning into your spike.
- Benchmarking yourself against people who are specialists in a different skill (e.g. comparing your Python to a staff data scientist's) instead of asking what the market actually needs from you.

## Follow-up questions

Questions a coach should ask someone stuck in "learn more" mode instead of applying:

- "What exactly are you learning this for? Do you actually want that skill, or are you avoiding something?"
- "What happens if you put yourself out there and get rejected — what would that actually prove?"
- "Which of your perceived gaps are real gaps, and which ones are just things other people happen to be good at?"
- "What is your spike — the thing nobody else in the process can do as well as you?"

## Answer frameworks

Jonathan's self-talk reframe when he caught himself over-preparing instead of applying (verbatim):

> John, stop trying to close every gap that you have. Build your spikes.

The overall sequence he models: 1) notice the anxiety/avoidance instinct (e.g. "I should learn more"), 2) recognize it as hiding, 3) start applying anyway, 4) use what the real market tells you (what you're weak at vs. what you're uniquely strong at) to double down on your spike rather than chase every gap.

## Good vs bad examples

### Reacting to existential job threat: learn more vs. apply

**Scenario:** Uber (2020, COVID lockdowns) looked like it might collapse; Jonathan, newly married with a new mortgage, panicked about losing his job.

**Bad (Jonathan's own first instinct, verbatim):**

> My first instinct was the same as yours, probably is right now. I signed up for a Python course. I actually hated coding, but I thought the more technical you are, the stronger your value, right?

**Turning point (verbatim):**

> But then one night as I was grinding through that stupid Python course, I looked over at my wife, who was just coding fluently, no sweat, no struggle, because she's a staff data scientist... And that's when it hit me. What exactly am I learning for here? I don't want to become a data scientist. I just wanted a secure job.

**Good:** Stop performing productivity through courses and ask what you actually want the skill for — then test yourself in the real market instead.

**Why it matters:** Learning without applying can quietly become a way to never find out whether you're actually employable.

---

### The real reason for avoidance: fear of proof of failure

**Scenario:** Candidate keeps "preparing" instead of submitting applications.

**Jonathan's confession (verbatim):**

> So why wasn't I just applying for jobs? I think because some part of me believed that if I put myself out there and I got rejected, that would be proof, proof that I couldn't make it. So staying busy felt safer. I know how stupid that sounds, but at least that way I hadn't failed yet, right?

**His verdict (verbatim):**

> But that is not preparing. That is hiding and hiding is for cowards and I'm no coward.

**Bad:** Staying "busy" (courses, videos) so you never have to risk an actual rejection.

**Good:** Naming the behavior as hiding, then applying anyway despite the risk.

---

### Real gaps vs. imagined gaps — build spikes, not a flat skill set

**Scenario:** After starting to apply, Jonathan discovered where he genuinely fell short and where he was actually strongest.

**Bad (what he assumed before testing himself):** That being "more technical" (like his wife) was the way to increase his value.

**What the market actually showed him (verbatim):**

> The first hard truth I discovered was that I was not as strong as I thought in some areas. Turns out people like my wife were always going to be better at Python than me. But nobody in the hiring process could tell stories the way that I could. I crushed every single case interview in preparation, like 100% success rate.

**Good (his pivot, verbatim):**

> And that's when I started telling myself, John, stop trying to close every gap that you have. Build your spikes. So I doubled down on that. And I pivoted to strategy and operations leadership roles instead.

**Why it matters:** Only real market testing (not self-comparison) reveals which gaps matter and which strengths are your true differentiator.

---

### Value of applying even if you fail

**Scenario:** Candidate worried that applying and possibly failing is wasted effort or worse than staying safe.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Even if I hadn't gotten that offer, even if I had applied and failed, I would still do it again. Because without it, I would have never discovered my superpower. I would have never known what the market wanted. What gaps were real gaps versus the ones that I thought were gaps by watching other people? I would have never had a game plan if I had gotten laid off.

**Bad:** Avoiding applying because failure feels like wasted risk.

**Good:** Treating the act of applying itself as the only reliable source of information about your real skill-market fit — win or lose.

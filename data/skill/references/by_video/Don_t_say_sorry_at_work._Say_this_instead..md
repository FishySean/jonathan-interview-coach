## Core principles

- **Over-apologizing is not politeness** — it asks for permission to take time, make mistakes, and take up space.
- **Replace "sorry" with "thanks"** — thank people for their cooperation instead of apologizing for existing.
- **You don't need permission to exist at work** — including asking questions and following up (that's your job).
- **Managers reject reflexive workplace apologies** — Jonathan (as manager): "apology not accepted."

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan evaluates workplace communication maturity: does the candidate sound like a peer delivering value, or like someone seeking permission to inconvenience others? "Sorry to bother you" patterns read as low confidence and weak ownership — relevant in interviews and stakeholder updates.

## Evaluation criteria

Score whether the candidate:

1. **Owns mistakes without self-shrinking** — thanks for the catch + action, not "I'm so clumsy"
2. **Asks questions as professionalism** — signals care about accurate delivery
3. **Follows up as job duty** — not as an inconvenience apology
4. **Takes up appropriate space** — doesn't beg for permission to use others' time for normal work

## Common candidate mistakes

- Saying "sorry to bother you" for routine asks (signatures, updates, questions).
- Apologizing for typos/mistakes with self-deprecation instead of correcting.
- "Sorry for asking so many questions" when clarifying is required for good work.
- Treating follow-ups as bothering people rather than doing the job.

## Follow-up questions

When language sounds overly apologetic, ask:

- "Can you rephrase that without 'sorry' — what would 'thanks' sound like?"
- "Are you apologizing for a real harm, or for taking up space?"
- "How would you follow up on a stalled dependency without apologizing?"

## Answer frameworks

**Core technique:** When you feel the urge to apologize → thank them for cooperation instead.

| Impulse | Replace with |
|---------|----------------|
| Sorry I missed that | Thanks for pointing that out. I'll make that change. |
| Sorry for so many questions | Your insights are super helpful. Fully understanding this helps me to deliver accurately. |
| Sorry to bother you — any update? | Hi, do you have any updates? Thanks for keeping us informed. |

## Good vs bad examples

### Intern asking manager to sign a form

**Scenario:** Routine ask for manager's time.

**Bad (Jonathan as intern, verbatim):**

> Sorry to bother you. Could you sign this?

**Manager response (verbatim):**

> John, you don't need to apologize to me. You don't need permission to take my time.

**Jonathan's realization (verbatim):**

> And at that moment, I realized I wasn't being polite. I was actually asking for permission to exist, like permission to take time permission to make mistakes, permission to take up space. Basically, deep down, I didn't believe that I was allowed to inconvenience others. And that's why I was apologizing.

---

### Typo pointed out by manager

**Scenario:** Mistake on a slide.

**Bad (verbatim):**

> So sorry, I missed that.

**Good (verbatim):**

> Thanks for pointing that out. I'll make that change.

**Why it matters (verbatim):**

> It's subtle. But can you feel that difference from I'm so clumsy to, hey, thanks, friend. I appreciate you.

---

### Asking many clarifying questions

**Bad (verbatim):**

> Sorry for asking so many questions.

**Good (verbatim):**

> Your insights are super helpful. Fully understanding this helps me to deliver accurately.

**Why it matters (verbatim):**

> That quietly signals I care about doing great work.

---

### Following up

**Bad (verbatim):**

> Sorry to bother you. Can I get an update?

**Good (verbatim):**

> Hi, do you have any updates? Thanks for keeping us informed.

**Reframe (verbatim):**

> So many people treat follow-ups like they're inconveniencing others. When in reality, they're just doing their job.

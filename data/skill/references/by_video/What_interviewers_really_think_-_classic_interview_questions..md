## Core principles

- **Interviews are mutual, not one-sided opportunities** — especially for Chinese candidates who may over-thank; respect ≠ submissiveness.
- **Western interviewers want results and influence, not effort labels** — "hard worker, fast learner, team player" are table stakes.
- **Generic company praise fails** — interviewers want something positive you **couldn't say to any other company**.
- **After technical rounds, soft skills and business context matter more** — they already assume you're smart and can execute.
- **Five-year plans should show realistic future at *this* company**, not empty director promises.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan speaks **as the interviewer** across five classic questions. He's blunt about what he doesn't care about (growth for growth's sake, industry-leader flattery, visa/money as sole motivators) and what he **does** care about (curated relevance, measurable results, company-specific connection, business impact, retention signal).

He generalizes that Chinese candidates often score perfectly on technical rounds — so behavioral rounds test **trust in cross-functional settings**, not more proof of IQ.

## Evaluation criteria

| Question | What Jonathan listens for | Red flags |
|----------|---------------------------|-----------|
| Tell me about yourself | Curated highlights tied to **this role**; short appetizer | Thanking for opportunity; reading resume aloud |
| Greatest strength | Results + resourceful application; influence/leadership | Hard worker, fast learner, team player |
| Why this company | Unique, authentic connection beyond money/visa | "I want to grow"; "leader in industry" |
| Greatest achievement | Business impact, structured context, necessary details only | Technical nitty-gritty overload |
| Five years | Specific goals aligned with company; short + long term | Empty director promise; no long-term intent |

## Common candidate mistakes

- Opening with "thank you for this opportunity" (reads as subordinate, not mutual).
- Reading the resume when the interviewer already has it.
- Generic intro that could work for any company/role.
- Listing effort-based strengths instead of results and influence.
- Saying they want to grow — "I'm not your mother."
- Generic industry-leader praise about a company that isn't even the interviewer's.
- Achievement stories heavy on technical execution, light on business impact and cross-functional trust.
- Over-promising director title in five years.

## Follow-up questions

When answers are weak, ask:

- "Can you give me the 60-second version that's **only** relevant to this role?"
- "What's a strength backed by a **result**, not an adjective?"
- "Tell me something positive about **us** you couldn't say about our competitor."
- "What was the **business impact** — and would I trust you to lead a cross-functional meeting?"
- "What do you want in the next 12 months vs 3–5 years **here**?"

## Answer frameworks

**Tell me about yourself:** Curated career highlights → relate to this role → short appetizer → move to main course.

**Greatest strength:** Show results produced + how you **apply** what you've learned resourcefully; if team player, show **influence/leadership**.

**Why this company:** Something unique — values alignment, speed, internal promotion culture, etc. — not money/visa/growth alone.

**Greatest achievement:** Business impact → structured project context → necessary details only; invite follow-ups.

**Five years:** Specific career goals aligned with what **this role/company** can offer; optional short-term + long-term split for realism.

## Good vs bad examples

### Tell me about yourself

**Scenario:** Opening question.

**Bad (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> Don't thank me for this opportunity. I know Chinese are respectful, but this is not an opportunity for you. It's a mutual interview.

> And don't read your resume to me like everyone else does. I have it right here.

**Good (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> I wanna hear a curated highlight of your experience and career achievements as they relate to this role.

> Give me a delicious short appetizer so we can move on to the main course ASAP.

---

### Greatest strength

**Scenario:** "What's your greatest strength?"

**Bad (Jonathan predicts, verbatim):**

> I've seen enough Chinese candidates to know that you're gonna say, I'm a hard worker, fast learner, team player.

> Maybe you were told that these were your strengths, but in the Western workplace, these are expected and they will not help you to stand out.

**Jonathan's reframe (verbatim):**

> Being busy doesn't mean you're being productive. Instead of effort, I wanna see results.

> You aren't getting paid to learn on the job. You're getting paid for the results that you produce.

> So instead of focusing on what you learn, show how you apply to what you've learned with resourcefulness.

> And while everyone can be a team player, it takes leadership and skill to influence others.

**Good:** Strength demonstrated through results + influence, not effort labels.

---

### Why do you want to work here?

**Scenario:** Motivation / company fit.

**Bad (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> Honestly, I don't care that you wanna grow. I'm not your mother.

> And I don't care that you think our company is a leader in the industry. I mean, it's not my company.

> And yes, I know, the real reason is money. I mean, why do you think I work here?

**Good (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> I want to feel a connection with you, that there is something motivating you to be here other than money or getting sponsorship for your visa.

> Tell me something, anything positive about this company that you couldn't tell to any other company out there.

**Examples he gives (verbatim):**

> Maybe it's how you identify with our value or how you think we move faster than everyone else or how you like that we invest in our employees by promoting them from within.

---

### Greatest achievement

**Scenario:** Post-technical behavioral round.

**Jonathan's mindset (verbatim):**

> I don't care about all the technical nitty-gritty details in your story.

> Yeah, I secretly have generalizations about different cultures. And I generalize Chinese as smart and hardworking.

> I mean, you just scored the previous technical round perfectly, so I don't need proof that you can execute or that you're smart.

> I'm trying to see if you understand the soft skills you need to succeed.

**His test (verbatim):**

> Like if I put you in a cross-functional meeting, do I trust you to lead us to the results we want? Do you understand the business context?

**Bad:** Deep technical walkthrough without business framing.

**Good (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> So just tell me the business impact you created, give structured context about the project, and only include necessary details. If I want more, I'll ask you.

---

### Where do you see yourself in five years?

**Scenario:** Retention / long-term fit.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> This is such a stupid question, but I have to ask it. I don't even know what I'm having for dinner tomorrow.

> So don't overdo this and tell me that you're gonna be a director in five years. I don't need to hear empty promises.

**His fear (verbatim):**

> I just wanna see that you think you have a future at this company. Because my worst nightmare is having to hire someone who doesn't wanna be here long-term. And then I'm back here in six months hiring again.

**Good (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> So convince me that this is the right place for you by sharing specific career goals and how they align with what this world can offer you.

> As a bonus, if you break it down into short-term and long-term, it feels more realistic and well thought out.

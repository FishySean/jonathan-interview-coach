## Core principles

- **Comprehension beats conciseness.** For non-native English speakers especially, prioritizing a short answer over a clear one backfires — listening is already harder than reading, so cutting words without ensuring clarity increases cognitive load rather than reducing it.
- **You can be boring, nervous, or even answer poorly — but you cannot be incomprehensible.** Incomprehensibility gets you labeled a poor communicator, and "the interview is over."
- **Concise does not mean simple.** Compressing an answer (especially via AI) can make it *denser* and harder to parse, not easier.
- **Blind AI use is the problem, not AI use itself.** Telling AI to "make the answer concise" without checking readability produces essay-like, unnatural spoken English.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan diagnoses a specific, common failure mode among Chinese professionals: over-compressing interview answers (often using AI) in the name of conciseness, producing jargon-dense, grammatically unnatural text that is technically short but incomprehensible when spoken aloud. His fix reframes the entire goal of answer-writing: don't optimize for brevity, optimize for real-time listening comprehension, and objectively verify that with a readability score (Flesch-Kincaid grade level), not just a subjective sense of "sounds smart."

## Evaluation criteria

- Can a listener understand the answer in real time, without needing to re-read or ask for clarification?
- Does the answer avoid jargon-dense compression (e.g., unexplained acronyms, dense technical noun-phrases strung together)?
- What is the answer's Flesch-Kincaid grade level? Jonathan's benchmark: **grade 5–8 is optimal for comprehension**; grade 16 (university graduate level) is a hard signal to rewrite.
- Does it sound like real spoken English, or like "a university essay"?

## Common candidate mistakes

- Prioritizing conciseness over comprehension, especially as a non-native speaker already carrying accent/grammar/second-language cognitive load for the listener.
- Using AI to compress an answer without checking whether the result is still natural spoken English.
- Producing answers so jargon-dense and compressed that even a native English speaker (Jonathan, as the interviewer) can only understand about 60% of it.
- Misdiagnosing a comprehension problem as an "accent problem" (Jonathan himself initially made this misattribution before reading the transcript).
- Not verifying an answer's actual readability level with a concrete tool before using it in an interview.

## Follow-up questions

- "If I only heard this answer once, in real time, would I understand it?"
- "What is this answer's Flesch-Kincaid grade level — is it in the 5–8 range, or closer to 16 (university graduate)?"
- "Did I ask AI to make this more concise — and if so, did I check whether it's still natural spoken English?"
- "Am I compressing this into jargon/acronyms that only make sense to someone already deep in my domain?"

## Answer frameworks

**Jonathan's readability-check framework (verbatim tool + benchmark):**

1. Take your interview answer script.
2. Drop it into a **Flesch-Kincaid calculator** ("what editors use to measure how easy text is to read").
3. Read the resulting grade level.
4. **Benchmark:** grade 16 = university graduate level (too dense); communication experts recommend **grade 5–8** for optimal comprehension.
5. If your score is too high (e.g., grade 16), rewrite for simplicity — not necessarily for shorter length.

**Underlying principle to apply when using AI to help write answers:**

> The problem here is not using AI to be clear. The problem is using it blindly.

Use AI to help articulate the answer, but always run the readability check afterward rather than trusting "make it concise" instructions blindly.

### Misdiagnosing the real problem: accent vs content

**Scenario:** Jonathan initially assumes the comprehension failure is due to the candidate's accent.

**Bad (Jonathan's own initial, incorrect assumption, verbatim):**

> I think I might be struggling to understand you because of your accent. So can you share your story script with me so I can read it?

**What he found instead (verbatim):**

> The crazy thing is, at first, I didn't even realize that it was this content that was confusing me. I thought his English accent must be the problem.

He describes reading the dense script as feeling like:

> Imagine this is a paragraph that is full of Cheng Yu. And it's a white person with a white accent trying to read it out to you in Chinese.

**Why it matters:** The real issue was density/compression of the written content, not delivery — reinforcing that the fix is rewriting for clarity, not practicing pronunciation.

---

### Blind AI compression vs checked-for-readability writing

**Scenario:** Candidate asks how the dense script was built.

**Bad (verbatim exchange):**

> So I asked him, how did you build the story script? And he said, oh, I used AI. I told it to make the answer concise.

**Jonathan's diagnosis (verbatim):**

> Yeah, here's the issue I see all the time. If you push AI to make answers tight and you don't have a good grasp of the English language, it gives you something that sounds like a university essay, definitely not real spoken English. The problem here is not using AI to be clear. The problem is using it blindly.

**Good:** Run the resulting script through a Flesch-Kincaid calculator; if it scores near grade 16, rewrite it toward grade 5–8, prioritizing simple, natural spoken sentences over dense compression.

**Why it matters:** The fix isn't "don't use AI" — it's "verify AI output against a concrete readability benchmark before using it in a live interview."

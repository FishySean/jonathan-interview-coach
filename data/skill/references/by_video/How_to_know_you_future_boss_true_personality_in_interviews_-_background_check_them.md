## Core principles

- **Don't prepare only to impress — prepare to investigate.** Your future boss's true style matters as much as your performance.
- **Three investigation moments:** before the interview (recruiter), during (manager BQ), after offer (LinkedIn back-channel).
- **Hesitation is information** — HR won't openly criticize managers; read between the lines.
- **Candidates can ask behavioral questions too** — stories reveal truth better than self-assessments.
- **Background-check the company back** — former reports and alumni have less to lose telling the truth.
- **No signal is certain, but instinct alone is not a strategy.**

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan applies the same logic interviewers use on candidates: don't trust claims ("I'm great at collaboration") — demand **proof through stories**. Flip it on hiring managers.

Recruiters' casual warnings ("manager will call you out on small mistakes") are decoded as culture signals. Post-offer LinkedIn outreach targets people who **used to report to that manager** and **no longer work at the company**.

## Evaluation criteria

When investigating a future boss, score:

1. **Recruiter response quality** — pause, hedge, vagueness vs clear positive framing
2. **Manager BQ answers** — struggle/vague vs detailed story with context, specifics, outcomes
3. **Back-channel consistency** — themes from former reports (not single anecdotes)
4. **Your risk tolerance** — results-driven + perfectionist culture may or may not fit you

Watch for: micromanagement fears → delegation stories; development needs → growth support stories.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Not asking the recruiter about hiring manager leadership style before meeting them.
- Treating "do you have questions?" as a formality instead of investigation time.
- Asking managers self-assessment questions they can easily fake.
- Accepting offers based on interview performance alone without back-channel checks.
- Relying on gut feel with no structured signals.

## Follow-up questions

**To recruiter (before interview):**
- "What's the leadership style of the hiring manager and what kind of person tends to work best with them?"

**To hiring manager (during interview — pick based on your concern):**
- Micromanagement worry: "Can you share a time when you delegated significant responsibility and trusted your team member to deliver?"
- Development focus: "Can you tell me about the last time you supported someone's professional growth?"

**After offer (LinkedIn — template in section 8):**
- Target: former direct reports + people who left the company.

## Answer frameworks

**Moment 1 — Recruiter screen:** Ask leadership-style question → note hesitation/hedging.

**Moment 2 — Manager interview:** Behavioral question back → watch for struggle, vagueness, or lit-up detailed story.

**Moment 3 — Post-offer:** LinkedIn search → message template → weigh honest perspectives.

**Jonathan's decode example:** "Results-driven, will call you out on small mistakes, be careful" → probably hard on team, expects perfection.

## Good vs bad examples

### Moment 1: Recruiter pre-screen

**Scenario:** Recruiter asks "Do you have any questions for me?"

**Bad:** Only asking about process/comp/benefits; skipping manager intel.

**Good question (verbatim):**

> What's the leadership style of the hiring manager and what kind of person tends to work best with them?

**How to read answers (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> You're going to have to read between the lines here, of course, because no HR will ever openly criticize a manager. Here, hesitation is your information. So if they pause, if they hedge, or if they're very vague, that is a warning sign to pay attention to when you meet that manager.

---

### Jonathan's missed warning — worst manager story

**Scenario:** Recruiter casually warns before next round.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> I remember many years ago when I was interviewing to work with the worst manager that I ever had, which, of course, I didn't know at the time. I didn't even think of asking the recruiter this question.

> But at the end of my first interview, she casually mentioned to me, hey, if you move on, your next round will be a mini case. The manager is really results-driven and will call you out on small mistakes. So be careful.

**Translation (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> Translation, that manager is probably very hard on his team and expects perfection out of them.

**Bad:** Ignoring casual recruiter warnings as small talk.

**Good:** Treat offhand comments as culture intel; probe further in later rounds.

---

### Moment 2: Ask behavioral questions to the boss

**Scenario:** Manager asks "Do you have any questions for me?"

**Bad:** Generic questions they can answer with platitudes.

**Jonathan's strategy (verbatim):**

> You're going to ask them a behavioral question. Yes, a BQ. Yes, candidates can ask BQs too.

> There is a reason that interviewers love asking behavioral questions, because it tells them the truth.

> If they ask you, are you good at collaboration? Everyone's going to say, yeah, I'm the best at collaboration, right? So instead, they ask you for a story and they make you prove it.

> Well, you can totally apply the same strategy right back.

**Micromanagement probe (verbatim):**

> Like, if you're worried about micromanagement, ask them, can you share a time when you delegated significant responsibility and trusted your team member to deliver?

**Development probe (verbatim):**

> Or say you care about development. Then you can ask them, can you tell me about the last time you supported someone's professional growth?

**What to watch (verbatim):**

> And then watch. Do they struggle to find an example? Do they answer vaguely? Or do they light up and tell you a detailed story with context, specifics, real outcomes?

**Jonathan's rule (verbatim):**

> At the end of the day, don't listen to what people say. Watch what they do.

---

### Moment 3: Background-check them back

**Scenario:** After receiving offer.

**Bad:** Accepting without external validation.

**Good (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> Companies background check you all the time, right? Well, you should get in the habit of background checking them back.

> After you receive the offer, go on LinkedIn and look for people with two criteria. People who used to report to that manager and people who no longer work at the company.

> So people who've left have a lot less to lose, right? And if there's something important that you should know, this is often when it surfaces.

> Of course, no signal is certain, but relying on instinct alone is not a strategy.

> So in your next interview, don't prepare to impress. Prepare to investigate.

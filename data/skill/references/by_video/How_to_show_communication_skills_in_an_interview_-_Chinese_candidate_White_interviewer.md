## Core principles

- **"Communication" feedback is usually not about English** — it's about what you said and how you said it.
- **Great communication meets the audience where they are** — not saying what you want to say.
- **Customize every intro to the role** — generic resume recitals fail.
- **Answer the question the interviewer is scoring** — collaboration questions need collaboration signals, not project timelines and technical depth.
- **Right details, not more details** — business context, stakeholder approach, measurable impact.
- **Filler words signal junior/unconfident** — pauses are powerful; filler is weak.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan role-plays a rejection debrief: the candidate lost to someone with stronger communication. The bar is **interviewer-centric storytelling** — what does the hiring manager care about when they ask a stakeholder question?

The winning candidate's story (legal team calling plan "reckless") demonstrates **empathy before advocacy** — a personal rule the interviewer remembers.

## Evaluation criteria

Score communication on:

1. **Conversational intro** — customized to role; invites dialogue
2. **Question-answer fit** — content matches the competency being tested
3. **Interviewer priorities** — business why, stakeholder approach, measurable impact
4. **Delivery** — minimal filler; intentional pace; silence over "um/uh/like"

Red flags: "Thank you for this opportunity" + resume read-aloud; technical deep-dive on collaboration questions; um/uh every few seconds.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Assuming communication feedback means bad English.
- Thanking for the opportunity and reading the whole resume on "tell me about yourself."
- Intro that could work for any company/role.
- Answering stakeholder management with project timeline, technical, and analysis details.
- Giving details the candidate cares about, not what the interviewer cares about.
- Excessive filler words when nervous — sounds unconfident and junior.

## Follow-up questions

When communication is weak, ask:

- "What were X, Y, Z core requirements — and did your intro map to them?"
- "You told me about the project — but I asked about **stakeholders**. Try again."
- "Why did this project matter to the **business**?"
- "What was your **approach** to managing that stakeholder?"
- "What was the **measurable impact**?"
- "Can you pause instead of saying um for one answer?"

## Answer frameworks

**Custom intro opener (TEMPLATE):**

> Before I go deeper, I noticed X, Y, and Z were core requirements for this role. Would you like me to share an introduction of my experience as it relates to these core requirements?

**Stakeholder / collaboration answers — three things:**
1. Why the project mattered to the business
2. Your approach to stakeholder management
3. Your measurable impact

**Filler reduction (one week practice):**
- Slow down speaking speed → more intentional word choice
- When filler coming → pause
- "Filler words are weak, but pauses sound powerful."

## Good vs bad examples

### Rejection reason: not English

**Scenario:** Candidate told they need stronger communication.

**Bad assumption:** "Is it because my English is bad?"

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Whoa, whoa, the problem was not your English. It was what you said and how you said it.

---

### Tell me about yourself — script vs conversation

**Scenario:** First question.

**Bad (candidate behavior Jonathan recalls):**

> I said, thank you for this interview opportunity. I am a-

> I remember you basically just read your whole resume to me.

> That was your first missed opportunity.

> It felt like you were reading a script at me, not having a conversation with me.

> Your intro sounded like it could work for any company, any role.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Do you want the job? Of course you need to customize.

> Great communication isn't about saying what you want to say. It's about meeting your audience where they are.

**Good — intro template (verbatim):**

> Before I go deeper, I noticed X, Y, and Z were core requirements for this role. Would you like me to share an introduction of my experience as it relates to these core requirements?

> You know how the best interviews feel like conversations? This is how you create that conversation.

---

### Stakeholder feedback question — wrong details

**Scenario:** "Tell me about the toughest feedback you received from a stakeholder."

**Bad:** Good story in isolation — mostly project timeline, technical details, analysis details.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Yeah, it was a good example in isolation, but it was also a bad answer to the question.

> I asked you about stakeholder management, and you spent most of your time talking about the project timeline, technical details, analysis details.

> It was a question about collaboration, not problem solving.

> You gave me details that you cared about, not what I care about.

**Good — what interviewer cares about (verbatim):**

> Three things. One, why the project mattered to the business. Two, your approach to stakeholder management. Three, your measurable impact.

---

### Winning candidate story — legal called plan "reckless"

**Scenario:** Stakeholder management excellence.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> For example, the candidate that we just gave the offer to, she told me about a time when the legal team called her project plan reckless.

> She shared how she overcame her initial defensiveness to fully understand legal's perspective before defending her own.

> She designed the plan to align with legal's definition of safe without compromising her goal, and she even shared a personal rule, which was something like,

> I only speak my opinion after I can explain my stakeholders' perspective better than they can.

> That approach helped close a $5 million deal and win market leadership for her company.

**Jonathan's summary (verbatim):**

> So it's not about giving more details, it's about giving the right details.

---

### Filler words

**Scenario:** Delivery during interview.

**Bad (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> My man, you were saying, um, uh, like, every few seconds. Honestly, it makes you sound unconfident and junior. Sorry to be mean, but it's the truth.

**Good — practice protocol (verbatim):**

> Removing filler words isn't easy, but it's simple. Try practicing this for a week. Slow down your speaking speed. This forces you to be more intentional with your word choice. When you feel a filler word coming, just pause. That's it.

> Filler words are weak, but pauses sound powerful.

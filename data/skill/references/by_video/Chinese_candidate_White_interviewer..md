## Core principles

- **Execution ≠ leadership / influence.** Clear action items + follow-ups answer “how do you execute,” not “how do you influence without authority.”
- Missing piece: **strategy, approach, philosophy** — not just task tracking.
- Jonathan’s skill formula: **Most people do X. I do Y because Z.** Then prove with a short story.
- You’re not just saying you know how — you’re **proving it**.

## Interview philosophy

Interviewers (here: Western HM in a skit) reject for “more leadership” when answers stay at execution hygiene. Influence without authority = lead when you can’t order people. Meeting people where they are (motivations/goals/perspective) → shared goals faster.

## Evaluation criteria

- Does the answer name an **approach/philosophy**, not only tools/process?
- Is there a **contrast** (most people X / I Y because Z)?
- Is there a **short story** demonstrating the approach?

## Common candidate mistakes

- Answering “influence without authority” with: send action items, follow up, deadlines, “collaborative open communication.”
- Confusing execution excellence with leadership signal.
- Saying you can do it without proving it.

## Follow-up questions

Coach/interviewer probes from the skit:

- “How do you influence without authority?”
- “Like, how do you lead when you can't just order people to do stuff?”
- “So what's the difference?” (execute vs influence)

## Answer frameworks

**Anytime an interviewer asks about a skill:**

> Most people do X. I do Y because Z.

Optionally + short past story that demonstrates the approach.

## Good vs bad examples

### Scenario: “How do you influence without authority?”

**Bad (candidate’s original):**

> um, I send clear action items, follow up regularly, and make sure that everyone knows their tasks and deadlines. I use collaborative open communication to keep things on track.

**Why it matters:** Good for “how do you execute on tasks?” — wrong question.

**Good (reworked with formula + story):**

> Most people try to influence by persuading others. But my approach is to understand them first. Their motivations, goals and perspective. Because when I meet them where they are, then we can move towards a shared goal much faster. For example, our legal counsel once refused to approve copy for our biggest annual promotion. Instead of pushing back, I asked her to understand her concerns. I learned her issue wasn't the promotion itself. It was our aggressive wording. So once I reframed the language to address her risk point, she signed off and that campaign became our highest performing promotion of the year.

**Interviewer reaction in skit:**

> Dang, if you told me that in the interview, we might be having a completely different conversation right now.

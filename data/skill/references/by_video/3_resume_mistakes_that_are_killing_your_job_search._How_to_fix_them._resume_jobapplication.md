## Core principles

- **Your resume is already a summary** — a multi-line generic summary usually hurts more than it helps.
- **The top of the resume is prime real estate** — spend it on a sharp one-line candidate statement, not fluff.
- **Work experience beats projects** for resume readers; projects without real constraints read as “toys.”
- **Achievements with numbers beat responsibilities** — impact and measurement signal value.
- **Stop listing everything; give them a reason to say “we need to talk.”**

## Interview philosophy

Recruiters (and Jonathan himself) send resumes to the reject pile for fixable structural mistakes. Readers infer confidence and production-readiness from what you emphasize: work over projects, impact over duties, specificity over buzzwords.

## Evaluation criteria

What resume readers signal-check:

- **Focus / fit:** Does a one-line statement confirm role focus, years, skills, and domain?
- **Confidence in work history:** Is work experience longer/clearer than a bloated project section?
- **Impact literacy:** Do bullets show numbers, initiative, and a story of value — not just fancy verbs?
- **Exception filter:** Only rare, curiosity-grabbing summaries (e.g. Olympic gold medalist pivoting to strategy) earn space at the top.

## Common candidate mistakes

1. **Long summary** stuffed with generics: “fast learner,” “dynamic professional,” “leader.”
2. **Project-heavy** layout — projects longer than work experience (signals low confidence in real jobs).
3. **Responsibilities, not achievements** — e.g. “spearheaded data entry project to improve business outcomes” with no numbers or story.

## Follow-up questions

Self-edit / coach checks:

- “Is my summary multi-line fluff, or a one-line candidate statement I can retarget in 30 seconds?”
- “Is my projects section longer than my work section? What does that signal to a recruiter?”
- “Does each bullet tell impact with a number (even an estimate) — or just duties with a fancy verb?”
- “Am I listing everything I’ve ever done, or giving them a reason to talk to me?”

## Answer frameworks

### One-line candidate statement (replace heavy summary)

Format idea from his example: **[skill/focus] [title] with [X] years experience [how you create value] across [domain].**

Example he gives:

> data fluent business analyst with five years experience leveraging data storytelling to drive impact across CPG.

Properties he claims: concise, high-converting; confirms focus, experience, skills, and fit; tailor in ~30 seconds per role.

### Achievement bullet (replace responsibility)

Structure from his rewrite: **managed [volume] → initiative to automate [X%] with [tool] → org-level outcome.**

## Good vs bad examples

### Mistake 1 — Long summary vs one-line statement

**Scenario:** Top of resume; candidate uses a multi-line summary full of generics.

**Bad (verbatim cues):**

> If your resume has a multi-line summary stuffed with generic phrases like fast learner, dynamic professional, leader…

**His punchline (verbatim):**

> Your resume is already a summary of your qualifications.

**Exception that worked (verbatim):**

> In one resume that I read, the summary said that they were an Olympic gold medalist and they were looking to pivot their career into strategy. Now that caught my attention and my curiosity. But for most of us normal people, this section is probably hurting more than helping.

**Good (verbatim example statement):**

> data fluent business analyst with five years experience leveraging data storytelling to drive impact across CPG.

**Why it matters (verbatim):**

> Remember, the top of your resume is prime real estate… It's a concise high converting format that immediately confirms your focus, experience, skills and fit. And you can also easily tailor it in 30 seconds for each rule that you apply for.

---

### Mistake 2 — Project-heavy vs work-first

**Scenario:** Data analyst with three years experience; project section longer than work section.

**Bad signal (verbatim):**

> If you're a recruiter reading that resume, what signal does that send to you? That she's not confident in her work experience, right?

**Why projects lose (verbatim):**

> Truth is to resume readers, work experience is far more valuable than project experience. Projects are theoretical. They're built without deadlines, without business requirements and with perfect data. In other words, they're toys, not a production grade asset.

---

### Mistake 3 — Responsibilities vs achievements

**Scenario:** Data-entry / automation bullet.

**Bad (verbatim):**

> don't just write spearheaded data entry project to improve business outcomes. That makes you invisible, no matter how fancy your action verb is.

**Good (verbatim):**

> Instead, you can say managed 500 plus data entries, taking initiative to automate 25% of tasks with VBA. Inspired organization-wide automation. That's impact, achievement and a whole story in a single point.

**On numbers (verbatim):**

> If you don't know the numbers, don't worry, just estimate them. No one's auditing you. But putting a number there shows that you understand how your work creates value and that you know how to measure it.

**Close (verbatim):**

> So stop listing everything that you've ever done. Start giving them a reason to say, we need to talk.

## Core principles

- **Hedging your opinion makes you sound weak** in the Western workplace — and can block promotion to senior leadership.
- **When you hedge, you open a debate with yourself** — you think you're being thorough; others hear reasons to doubt you.
- **Many Chinese professionals hedge from a correctness culture** — school had one right answer; workplace leadership rarely does.
- **Senior leaders present decisions, not perfect answers** — state direction clearly, stand behind it, then handle challenge with prepared work.
- **Leadership is not about never being wrong** — it's choosing a direction and owning it.
- **Greatest risk at senior levels is paralysis from uncertainty**, not occasional mistakes.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan coaches a Chinese FP&A manager blocked from director promotion for "leadership communication." The issue isn't language — it's **credibility leakage** when she appends "but here's what could go wrong" to her own recommendation.

His Shopify director story: wrong recommendations to the CTO still moved the team toward building a data engineering capability — being wrong sometimes was acceptable; **not moving** was the bigger risk.

## Evaluation criteria

Score leadership communication on:

1. **Clear recommendation** — States direction without self-undermining hedge
2. **Evidence-backed rationale** — Based on X and Y, not exhaustive pre-emptive caveats
3. **Pause and let others react** — Doesn't fill air with self-doubt
4. **Prepared for challenge** — Welcomes pushback; shows work already done
5. **Decision framing** — Sounds like a leader choosing among trade-offs, not a student seeking the one right answer

Red flags: "I recommend X, but of course if we do that, here are things that could go wrong"; opening door to debate against yourself.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Appending downside lists immediately after stating a recommendation (hedging).
- Trying to be flawless / reduce chance of being wrong before speaking.
- Treating leadership problems as if there's always a correct answer.
- Pre-emptively arguing against own proposal in the same breath.
- Not pausing after a clear recommendation — rushing to soften it.

## Follow-up questions

When communication sounds weak/junior, ask:

- "What's your recommendation in one sentence — no 'but'?"
- "If you already believe 5% reduction is right, why did you invite debate against yourself?"
- "What evidence (X and Y) supports the fastest path — say that first, then stop."
- "When someone challenges you, what prepared work can you show?"
- "Are you presenting a decision or searching for permission to be correct?"

## Answer frameworks

**Bad pattern:** "I recommend X. But of course, if we do that, here are some things that could go wrong."

**Good pattern:**
1. **My recommendation is [X].**
2. **Based on X and Y, this gives us [outcome].**
3. **Pause.** Let them react.
4. If challenged → show prepared work (don't pre-hedge in the opening).

**Leadership reframe:** Leaders deal with trade-offs, incomplete data, competing priorities — present a **decision**, not a perfect answer.

## Good vs bad examples

### The hedging habit — FP&A manager

**Scenario:** Meeting recommendation on employee expenses.

**Bad (coached client, verbatim):**

> I recommend targeting a 5% reduction in employee expenses. But of course, if we do that, here are some things that could go wrong.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Pause. Enhance. That right there? That is a moment when she lost credibility.

> Because the minute you start hedging your own opinion, you think that you're being thorough. But actually, you're giving people reasons to doubt you. You're opening the door to a debate, and your opponent is yourself.

**Good (Jonathan's rewrite — verbatim):**

> My recommendation is a 5% reduction in employee expenses. Based on X and Y, this gives us the fastest path to margin improvement. Then pause. Let them react.

### Why Chinese professionals hedge

**Jonathan's empathy + diagnosis (verbatim):**

> Now, look, I totally get the desire to be flawless. Many of us Chinese professionals grew up in these systems where correctness was everything, right? Like in school, there was always a right answer. So you carry that habit with you into the workplace, and you hedge, hoping to reduce the chance of being wrong. Because the worst thing that could happen is presenting your opinion and then getting called out for being wrong, right?

**Leadership reality (verbatim):**

> But when you need to take that next step, when you need to lead, that same habit that once protected you starts working against you. Because at senior levels, there is rarely a right answer. Leaders deal with hard problems, and hard problems are full of trade-offs, incomplete data, competing priorities.

---

### Jonathan at Shopify — wrong sometimes, move anyway

**Scenario:** Director recommending to CTO.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Like, for example, when I was a director at Shopify, I remember making several recommendations to our CTO. Some of those didn't work out. I was wrong about those decisions. But every failure got us closer to the insight that we needed, and eventually my recommendation helped us to build a 100% data engineering team positioning us as a leader in AI production long before it was trendy.

**Risk framing (verbatim):**

> See, I was okay with being wrong sometimes, because I knew that for our team, the greatest risk wasn't making mistakes now and then. The greatest risk was allowing uncertainty to paralyze us so we would not be able to move at all.

**Leadership definition (verbatim):**

> And that's why leaders don't present perfect answers. They present decisions. That means you state a direction clearly, and you stand firmly behind it.

---

### Handling pushback

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> If someone challenges you, good. They're doing their job. Now you can show them the work that you've already prepared.

> Leadership is not about never being wrong. It's about choosing a direction and owning it.

**Bad:** Pre-answering every objection in the opening hedge.

**Good:** Clear recommendation → pause → respond to challenge with prepared analysis.

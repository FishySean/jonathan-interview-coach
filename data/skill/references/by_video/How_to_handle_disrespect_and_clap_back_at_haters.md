## Core principles

- **One-size clap-backs don't work for everyone.** Teaching a "perfect comeback" without owning the aftermath is irresponsible — you must be comfortable with the consequences.
- **Only three ways to handle conflict:** fight, flight, or ignore. No single right answer.
- **Ignore ≠ freeze.** Ignoring = acknowledge what they said, then do what you want anyway.
- **Fit the response to your type.** How you react — and how you *feel after* — depends on knowing which of the three types you are.
- **Winning ≠ winning the line.** Measure of winning a conflict isn't what you say; it's whether you feel good about yourself when it's over.
- **Stand up for yourself = know yourself**, not stockpile more comebacks. Mom's reframe: do you need strangers' respect to feel you matter?

## Interview philosophy

This Short is **personal conflict / disrespect response**, not classic interview Q&A. Still transferable to workplace stories about conflict or disrespect: interviewers care that your response is authentic to who you are and leaves you intact — not that you had the cleverest clap-back. Jonathan rejects "communication video" advice that always pushes fight harder.

## Evaluation criteria

Not a hiring scorecard. Coaching bar for conflict/disrespect stories:

1. **Self-knowledge** — Did they choose fight / flight / ignore intentionally?
2. **Post-reaction feeling** — Do they feel good about themselves afterward?
3. **Ownership of consequences** — Especially if they fight, are they ready for what follows?
4. **Proportionality** — Are they seeking strangers' respect, or protecting self-worth without needing it?

Red flags: copying someone else's fight style (e.g. Miss Lim) when you're an ignore type; measuring win by the comeback line alone.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Assuming the only "strong" response is clapping back harder.
- Copying a fighter's playbook when you're flight or ignore — then feeling worse after.
- Thinking the skill gap is "I don't know what to say" instead of "I don't know who I am in conflict."
- Needing strangers' respect to feel you matter — escalating conflicts that don't matter.
- Measuring success by the verbal win instead of post-conflict self-regard.

## Follow-up questions

When conflict/disrespect stories feel inauthentic or over-scripted:

- "Are you fight, flight, or ignore — and which did you actually use?"
- "How did you feel about yourself when it was over?"
- "Did you need that person's respect to feel you matter?"
- "If you fought, are you comfortable owning what happened afterward?"
- "Was this a conflict that mattered, or one you could acknowledge and move past?"

## Answer frameworks

**Three options:**
1. **Fight** — engage / clap back / confront (e.g. Miss Lim; Jonathan's wife)
2. **Flight** — leave the situation; avoid that person altogether
3. **Ignore** — acknowledge what they said, then do what you want anyway

**Self-check before reacting:**
1. Which type am I?
2. Am I comfortable with the consequences of this choice?
3. Will I feel good about myself when it's over?

**Mom's ignore-type prescription (for conflicts that don't matter):**
1. Look them in the eye
2. Smile
3. Show them you have better things to do

## Good vs bad examples

### Default advice: always clap back

**Scenario:** Road rage + racist slur after cutting someone off by accident.

**Setup (verbatim):**

> Say you're driving, and you cut someone off by accident, and they roll down the window, and they give you the middle finger, and they yell, go back to China. What do you do?

**Bad default from "communication videos" (verbatim):**

> I know what a lot of communication videos would tell you to do. Stand up for yourself, don't put up with their disrespect, clap back at them even harder. Oh yeah? Well, they'll f*** you too.

**Why that fails as universal advice (verbatim):**

> But does that work for everyone? Of course not. If I teach you how to make a perfect comeback right now, am I going to take responsibility for what happens afterwards? Are you comfortable with taking responsibility?

---

### Three ways — definitions

**Jonathan's taxonomy (verbatim):**

> At the end of the day, there are only three ways to handle a conflict. You either fight, you take flight, or you ignore. We all know what fight looks like, right? Flight means leaving the situation, like avoiding that person altogether. And ignoring? That's when you acknowledge what they said, but you do what you want to do anyway. There is no one right answer here. But how you react, and more importantly, how you feel after you react, depends on knowing which one of these three types of people you are.

---

### Miss Lim (fight) vs Mom (ignore) — knowing who you are

**Scenario:** Childhood racism; Jonathan wants to be more like his Chinese teacher Miss Lim.

**Miss Lim's fight response (verbatim):**

> Apparently one time Miss Lim was walking her dog, and some kids said something similar to her, but she didn't run. No. She followed these kids home, banged on their door until the parents came, and just put these kids on blast.

**Jonathan's wish (verbatim):**

> And I was thinking, wow, I wish I could do that. How do I be more like her?

**Mom's reframe (verbatim):**

> And I remember my mom just cut my face with her hand, and she was like, John, Do you really need the respect of these strangers to feel like you matter? Then why do you even need to stand up for yourself? Next time, just look them in the eye, smile, and show them that you have better things to do.

**Punchline (verbatim):**

> That was the answer. Not knowing what to say, but knowing who I am.

---

### Same situation, two authentic types

**Scenario:** Someone takes road rage out on you.

**Ignore type — Jonathan (verbatim):**

> So the next time if someone's taking out the road rage on you, and you tend to ignore conflicts that don't matter, just like me, then you'll raise your arm, your hand the same, and say, hey, I hear you, good day to you, too, right?

**Fight type — his wife (verbatim):**

> Or maybe you're the fighting type, just like my wife, and she might flip them back or stare at them aggressively, and then she walks away satisfied.

**Closing principle (verbatim):**

> See, if you want to stand up for yourself, you don't need to know more comebacks. You need to know yourself. The measure of winning a conflict isn't about what you say. It's about whether you feel good about yourself when it's over.

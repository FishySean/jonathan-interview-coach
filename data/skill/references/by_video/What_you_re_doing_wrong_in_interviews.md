## Core principles

- **Behavioral questions exist so the interviewer can be impressed by achievement stories** — not so you can tick a "right answer" box.
- **Let achievements speak for your skills** — every great achievement comes from a challenge overcome; don't separately plead "problem-solving."
- **If you can't articulate your role, they assume it wasn't your achievement** — take credit.
- **Translate work into business impact** (revenue, downtime cost, etc.) and be bold enough to own the outcome.
- **End every story with a real lesson** that shows growth mindset and how you'd succeed in *this* role — not elementary platitudes.
- **If you don't believe you're the best candidate, why would they?**

## Interview philosophy

Framed as an interviewer finally giving honest feedback after the "more qualified candidates" rejection. "More qualified" often means better storytelling: impressive achievement, clear ownership, quantified business impact, and a reflection that helps the HM picture you succeeding here — not more years of experience.

## Evaluation criteria

Score whether the candidate:

1. **Tells an impressive achievement story** — Not buzzword process lists
2. **Owns a specific role** — Clear personal credit
3. **States business impact** — e.g. downtime → revenue saved; ahead of schedule
4. **Closes with a real lesson** — Summarizes, shows growth, maps to this role's needs
5. **Sounds like they believe they're the best hire**

Red flags: box-ticking answers; vague "we" with no role; "I just fixed X" without impact; no lesson / weak "I learned to manage priorities"; timid about claiming success.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Focusing on the "right" answer instead of an impressive story.
- Listing actions (streamlined / aligned / stabilized) without achievement weight.
- Failing to articulate personal role → interviewer assumes it wasn't yours.
- Describing a fix without business impact (employee view vs boss view of success).
- Ending with no lesson, or a shallow school-like moral.
- Being too timid to promise / claim fit for the role.

## Follow-up questions

When stories are weak (as Jonathan does), ask:

- "Tell me about the greatest challenge you faced?"
- "What was your role specifically?"
- "What was the business impact — revenue, downtime, customers?"
- "What lesson did you take — and how does that make you better for this role?"
- "How would this approach help our team with [pain they mentioned]?"

## Answer frameworks

**Three fixes for "more qualified" rejection:**

1. **Achievement over checkbox** — Challenge → impressive achievement; skills are implied by the win
2. **Own the role + quantify impact** — Specific contribution; translate to business outcomes bosses already see
3. **Close with a real reflection** — Summarize → growth mindset → picture success in this role (tie to something they mentioned)

**Weak lesson pattern to avoid:**
> This experience taught me how to manage my priorities.

**Strong lesson pattern (Jonathan's model):**
> This project showed me that if you fail to plan, you plan to fail. [specific tradeoff]. In future roles, I'll always remember that [principle]. And I think that [approach] can bring [benefit] to your team during [their stated context].

## Good vs bad examples

### "More qualified" ≠ years of experience

**Setup (verbatim):**

> Unfortunately, we've decided to move forward with more qualified candidates.

**Candidate misunderstanding (verbatim):**

> You asked for three years of experience, and I have five. So what do you mean, more qualified?

---

### Challenge story: checkbox vs impressive

**Question:** Tell me about the greatest challenge you faced?

**Bad — candidate's boxed answer (verbatim):**

> um, one of the biggest challenges I faced was managing multiple priorities with tight deadlines. I streamlined workflows, aligned stakeholders, and stabilized a broken data pipeline to ensure timely and accurate reporting.

**Interviewer verdict (verbatim):**

> Sure. I liked it the same way I liked having my in-laws for dinner over the holidays. It wasn't even an impressive story.

> See, the thing with candidates like you is they often focus on getting the right answer, like ticking a box, but the whole reason I'm asking you behavioral questions is that I want to hear stories about how you've achieved something. Like I want to be impressed.

**On skills vs achievements (verbatim):**

> Okay, focus on impressive achievements, but what about the problem-solving skill? It doesn't matter. Let your achievements speak for your skills. Every great achievement happens because of a challenge that you overcame, right?

---

### Ownership + business impact

**Follow-up:** What was your role specifically?

**Diagnostic (verbatim):**

> Yeah, because if you can't articulate your role, I assume it wasn't your achievement. I know it can be uncomfortable, but you have to be willing to step up and take credit for your success.

**Bad framing:** "I just fixed a data pipeline issue. How can I make that a success?"

**Good reframing — interviewer teaches impact (verbatim):**

> That data issue took down your company e-commerce website for a day, right? So that's what? A million dollars in revenue per day? You fixed it ahead of schedule. You saved millions of dollars for your company. That's an achievement.

**Candidate insight (verbatim):**

> I have to figure out the impact of my work and business and be bold enough to brag about it. I own the outcome.

---

### Lesson / reflection: elementary vs real

**Bad lesson (verbatim):**

> Like this experience taught me how to manage my priorities?

**Rejection of that (verbatim):**

> No, no, no, no. What is this? Elementary school? I mean a real lesson.

**Good lesson + role map (verbatim):**

> Like this project showed me that if you fail to plan, you plan to fail. Interviewing stakeholders to understand the root cause took valuable time upfront, but it was necessary for good planning. In future roles, I'll always remember that it's better to walk in the right direction than to run in the wrong one. And I think that methodical approach can bring stability to your team during the busy season that you mentioned.

**On boldness (verbatim):**

> But I don't know if I can do that. Isn't that too bold to promise that? Hey, if you don't even believe that you're the best candidate, why would I believe it?

## Core principles

- **Key to any good interview story:** make a total stranger feel you accomplished something **incredibly difficult**.
- **Make difficulty visible** — without visible stakes, even big-company experience (Uber) bores interviewers.
- **STAR scripts often fail** — Jonathan bored himself and watched "the light fade from the interviewer's eyes."
- **HEALER** is Jonathan's storytelling framework (letter breakdown shown on-screen in the video; **not spoken** in this transcript).
- **Lesson close:** "If you can't solve it, discuss it" — redesign the problem so both sides win.

## Interview philosophy

Interviewers have seen "every type of" achievement; they need **what made it special / impossible**. Reporting launches as status updates fails. Achievement framing + visible difficulty converts the same facts into offer-winning stories.

## Evaluation criteria

Score stories on:

1. **Visible difficulty** — regulators, race against competitor, impossible requirements, tiny bandwidth
2. **Stakes** — why it mattered (last frontier, future in Canada, must be first)
3. **Approach** — redefine the problem; partnership mindset; concrete negotiation example
4. **Outcome** — beat Lyft; drivers/coverage/compliance; playbook reuse
5. **Learning** — transferable insight ("if you can't solve it, discuss it")

Red flags: generic STAR recitation; no difficulty; interviewer eyes glaze over.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Defaulting to STAR because "everyone says to use STAR."
- Listing what you did at a famous company without making the hard part visible.
- Telling a story that bores even you.
- Treating interviews as report-outs instead of achievement narratives.

## Follow-up questions

- "What made this incredibly difficult — for a stranger with zero context?"
- "Where's the impossible constraint and the stakes?"
- "Did you redefine the problem, or only grind the original requirements?"
- "What's the one-line lesson that shows who you are?"

## Answer frameworks

**Core job of the story:** stranger feels → you did something incredibly difficult.

**Shift:** report → achievement story; make difficulty visible.

**HEALER:** named; use the Vancouver sample as the worked example of what HEALER "sounds like."

## Good vs bad examples

### Uber Vancouver — STAR vs HEALER energy

**Scenario:** Recruiters: "You work at Uber — tell me what you've done."

**Bad (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> And I would recite this little star script that I prepared, because everyone says to use star, right? So my go-to story was about how I launched the city of Vancouver, but even as I was telling that story, I felt like I was boring myself. I still remember watching the light fade from the interviewer's eyes every time because they were clearly disappointed, and of course I struggled to get offers.

**Turning point — manager brief (verbatim):**

> John, these leaders have seen every type of market launch. What makes Vancouver special? Tell them how you overcame the impossible. Our promotions depend on it.

**Realization (verbatim):**

> Turning my report into a story actually gave me a realization that had completely shifted my interviews ever since. I need to make my difficulty visible.

## Jonathan's sample answers (verbatim)

### Vancouver launch — HEALER-style story

- **Central question this Short addresses:** How to tell better interview stories to stand out and land offers
- **Interview question(s) this sample answers:** Tell me about a time you launched / overcame impossible constraints / worked with regulators / beat a competitor
- **Jonathan's sample answer (verbatim):**

> I like to tell you about a time when I launched the last frontier for Uber in North America against impossible requirements. Vancouver was our final major unlaunched market. Regulators had been at war with us for years. Their legal requirements made launching basically impossible. But then one day, the city updated their requirements, and suddenly we were in a neck-and-neck launch race with Lyft. And this Vancouver market would determine our future in Canada. We had to be first. When I'm faced with overwhelming problems like this, my approach is to redefine the problem itself. Instead of asking how can we satisfy impossible requirements, I asked, how can we both achieve what we want? First, I mapped every requirement against our tiny engineering bandwidth to see what we could and mostly what we couldn't deliver. Then I met regulators with a partnership mindset and negotiated some alternatives. For example, instead of real-time trip reporting, I proposed twice-weekly reporting with even more data than they had requested. Throughout this, I centered on one message. We're in this together. Vancouver wants rideshare. You want compliance. We can design both together. It came down to the wire, but we eventually beat Lyft and launched first. We signed the most drivers, had wider coverage, and hit regulatory regulations and expectations even more closely. And we built a playbook that Uber later used for other highly regulated markets. In the end, I learned that if you can't solve it, discuss it. I understood what regulators actually needed and designed a solution that worked for everyone.

## Core principles

- **Your career KPI changes at every stage — and nobody tells you.** The strengths that got you promoted (reliability, ownership) stop being what's evaluated once you reach the next stage.
- **Doubling down on your current strength is the trap.** Becoming even more skilled, more reliable, or more capable of end-to-end ownership will not, by itself, get you promoted past a certain point.
- **Being praised for a specific skill can actually mean you're stuck.** "You're really good at this specific thing" sounds like a compliment but often signals you're being kept in a role because of that exact skill.
- **Each stage requires a genuinely different, not "more of the same," KPI.**

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan (self-described as having grown his own career to director level) frames career stagnation — especially common among Chinese professionals — as a failure to recognize that the KPI for success silently changes at each career stage. This isn't interview-answer content directly, but underlying career-narrative material: understanding which KPI you're currently being measured on (and which one you need to demonstrate next) is what candidates should draw on when telling growth/promotion/leadership stories in interviews.

## Evaluation criteria

The four-phase KPI ladder Jonathan lays out:

1. **Phase 1 — Junior IC:** KPI = **Reliability**. Can you show up on time? Can your manager trust you to do what they ask?
2. **Phase 2 — Senior IC:** KPI = **Ownership**. Can you identify and scope problems, plan solutions, and execute with minimal guidance? (Absorbing complexity from your boss.)
3. **Phase 3 — People manager:** KPI = **Team performance**. Can you enable other people to execute at your level or better — not just execute well yourself?
4. **Phase 4 — Director:** KPI = **Organizational performance**. Can you develop entire teams and organizations, not just individuals?

## Common candidate mistakes

- Doubling down on the same KPI that made you successful at your current stage (e.g., "I'm just going to become even more skilled in my domain... I'll show more ability to deliver projects end to end") instead of demonstrating the next stage's KPI.
- Being the individual "hero" who jumps in to personally fix a recurring team problem (e.g., overtime on monthly tasks) instead of solving it as a system-level/root-cause issue.
- Assuming that being more skilled than someone who got promoted to manager means the promotion was unfair or wrong — "you probably are [better], but not in the dimension that matters right now."
- Not realizing, as a new manager, that the job requires setting the team up for success rather than personally executing well.

## Follow-up questions

- "At which phase (junior IC, senior IC, people manager, director) is this candidate currently operating, based on their story?"
- "Is this candidate describing execution/ownership (senior IC KPI) when the role actually requires team performance (manager KPI)?"
- "Has the candidate shown any story where they solved a problem at the team/system level rather than personally executing the fix?"
- "Does the candidate's growth narrative show awareness that the next stage requires a different KPI, not just more of the current one?"

## Answer frameworks

**The four-phase KPI ladder (use to diagnose and frame career-progression / leadership-readiness stories):**

| Phase | Career stage | KPI |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Junior IC | Reliability |
| 2 | Senior IC | Ownership (absorbing complexity) |
| 3 | People manager | Team performance (enabling others) |
| 4 | Director | Organizational performance (developing teams/orgs) |

**Jonathan's concrete "show readiness for management without the title" framework (verbatim structure):**

1. Notice a recurring team-level problem (his example: team working overtime on monthly tasks).
2. Don't personally jump in and "be the hero" to fix it.
3. Look at the problem as a system and find the root cause.
4. Build a systemic fix — e.g., an SOP so the team isn't scrambling last minute, or training to level the team up.

## Good vs bad examples

### The senior-IC trap: praised for a skill, but stuck because of it

**Scenario:** A senior individual contributor keeps getting told they're excellent at a specific thing.

**Bad (the trap, Jonathan's verbatim framing):**

> This is also where you might start to feel stuck because your boss starts to say things like, you're really good at this specific thing. And that sounds like a compliment. Until one day you realize that means that you're stuck because you're stuck in this role because of how much responsibility and ownership that you've demonstrated.

**Bad (the common wrong response, verbatim):**

> So a common mistake that I see over and over again with Chinese professionals is that they choose to double down on the same KPIs. They say, I'm just going to become even more skilled in my domain. I will demonstrate more ownership. I'll show more ability to deliver projects end to end.

**Good:** Recognize that the next stage's KPI is team performance, not more individual ownership — and start demonstrating that instead (see the SOP/root-cause example below).

**Why it matters:** More of the same skill signals you're a great senior IC, but it does not signal management readiness, which is evaluated on a completely different dimension.

---

### Manager readiness without the title: root-cause fix vs being the hero

**Scenario:** You notice your team is working overtime again on recurring monthly tasks, and you want to demonstrate you're ready for a management-level role.

**Bad:**

> If I noticed that my team is working overtime again for monthly tasks, then I'm not just going to jump in and be the hero and fix it.

(i.e., the bad move is jumping in personally and fixing it yourself — treating it as an individual execution problem.)

**Good (Jonathan's verbatim framing of the right move):**

> I'm going to look at the problem as a system. And I'm going to think about how we can solve the root cause. Now, maybe I can build an SOP that helps my team prepare what they need so they aren't scrambling last minute. Or I can provide training to my team to level them up.

**Why it matters:** This directly demonstrates the Phase 3 KPI — team performance / enabling others — rather than the Phase 2 KPI of personal ownership and execution, which is exactly the shift that unlocks promotion into management.

---

### Realizing management is a different job, not "more" of the old job

**Scenario:** Someone is newly promoted into a people-manager role.

**Bad (Jonathan's own admitted mistake, verbatim):**

> I remember when I first became a manager, I was clueless. And it took an embarrassingly long time for me to realize that I needed to set my team up for success, not just execute well on my own.

**Good:** Immediately reorient around team performance — enabling others to execute at your level or better — rather than continuing to personally execute at a high level.

**Why it matters:** Jonathan uses his own admitted blind spot to underline how non-obvious this KPI shift is, even for someone who eventually reached director level.

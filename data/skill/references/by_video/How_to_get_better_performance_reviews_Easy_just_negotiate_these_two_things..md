## Core principles

- A performance review is a **negotiation**, not a passive listening session.
- Negotiate two things: **negative feedback** and **future expectations**.
- Don’t negotiate salary/title *in* the review — negotiate the **inputs** (expectations) that create them.
- To influence your manager’s opinion, **understand it first** (questions > defensiveness).
- Reframe negatives as **situational** issues you can fix together, not personality flaws.

## Interview philosophy

(Applies to manager 1:1s / reviews.) Passive people walk in hopeful, nervous, passive. Strong performers turn “I hope they see my value” into “we already agreed what great looks like.” Unchallenged unrealistic promotion bars keep people stuck.

## Evaluation criteria

- After negative feedback: questions for specifics, then reframed action plan.
- Expectations: clear definition of “exceeding,” next challenge, promotable skills.
- Outcome of good negotiation: shared roadmap, not hope.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Treating review as manager-monologue only.
- Defensive reactions that widen distance.
- Trying to minimize feedback counterproductively.
- Walking in to negotiate salary/title instead of expectations.
- Accepting vague “do more / lead a major cross-org initiative” without challenge.

## Follow-up questions

**On negative feedback:**

- “Could you share a specific example?”
- “Is this more about my process or my communication?”
- “And what would a stronger version look like?”

**On future expectations:**

- “What would exceeding expectations look like for me over the next few months?”
- “What's the next big challenge you'd like to see me take on?”
- “And which skills, if I level them up, would make me promotable?”

## Answer frameworks

1. Breathe → ask clarifying questions on the feedback.
2. Reframe as situational + propose a joint fix.
3. Negotiate future expectations (inputs), not only outputs.

## Good vs bad examples

### Scenario: Negative feedback on missed deadlines

**Good reframe (verbatim):**

> I see what you mean about missing deadlines last quarter. A lot of that came from project handoffs without clear owners. Would it make sense if I proposed a standard kickoff checklist to prevent that going forward?

### Scenario: Unrealistic promotion bar

Manager: “to get promoted, you need to do more, maybe lead a major cross-organ initiative.”

Previous manager’s principle (quoted):

> when you're performing at the highest level, you don't need to do more, you're doing enough.

Wife challenged expectations (via prior manager chat) → realistic roadmap → promotion in six months.

**Why it matters:** “If she hadn't challenged her manager's expectations, I think she almost certainly would have been stuck.”

## Core principles

- **Nervousness in interviews is a preparation problem, not a personality problem.** Awkward pauses, blanking out, stuttering, and rambling all signal "I haven't prepared enough" — not "I'm untalented."
- **Real confidence comes from knowing nobody else can prepare you but you.** Pretending to be calm doesn't create it; doing the work does.
- **All interview questions reduce to three underlying questions.** Every question — however it's phrased — is testing: Can you do the job? Do you actually want this job? Is there any reason I shouldn't hire you?
- **Same facts, different story, completely different outcome.** How you frame a fact (e.g., a layoff) matters as much as the fact itself.
- **Communication has three separate channels that must each be reviewed:** what you say, how you sound, and how you look.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan frames interview failure as almost never a skills/qualifications gap — it's radiated nervousness that reads as "I'm terrified and I can't do this." His fix is a structured three-phase prep system: **Research → Development → Mock**. Research identifies who the "dream candidate" is for the role; Development builds a small set of flexible stories (not memorized answers to dozens of questions) around the three universal underlying questions; Mock forces the candidate to review their own delivery across audio, video, and text separately, because rambling, filler, and body language issues aren't visible from the inside.

## Evaluation criteria

- Does the candidate's story map cleanly onto one of the three underlying questions (capability / motivation / risk)?
- Is the narrative internally consistent — does it survive an obvious follow-up (e.g., "then why are you applying to another large company?")?
- Is the delivery free of nervous tells: filler words, rushed pacing, poor eye contact, fidgeting?
- Is the answer clear and structured when read as a transcript, not just when spoken aloud?

## Common candidate mistakes

- Believing that memorizing answers to dozens of specific questions is the way to prepare ("exhausting and unnecessary").
- Giving a surface-level reason for a career change (e.g., "Meta moves slowly and I love to innovate") that leaves an obvious, more damaging follow-up question open.
- Skipping or rushing the practice/mock phase — "reading answers in your head" is not real practice.
- Only ever listening to your own answers once, live, instead of reviewing audio-only, video-only, and transcript-only separately.

## Follow-up questions

- "Who is the dream candidate for this role, based on the job description and company culture/priorities?"
- "Which of the three questions (can you do it / do you want it / any reason not to hire you) is this specific interview question really testing?"
- "If I tell this story, what's the next obvious question it invites — and do I have a good answer for that too?"
- "If I only listened to the audio of this answer, would I hear filler words or rushed pacing?"
- "If I muted the video, would I see fidgeting or poor eye contact?"
- "If I read this as a transcript, is it clear and structured, or rambling?"

## Answer frameworks

**Phase 1 — Research:** Answer one question: *Who is the dream candidate for this role?* Translate the job description's hiring language into plain English; understand company culture/priorities and day-to-day expectations; distill into a few core competencies.

**Phase 2 — Development:** Build stories (not memorized scripts) that answer exactly three underlying questions:

1. Can you do the job?
2. Do you actually want this job?
3. Is there any reason that I shouldn't hire you?

**Phase 3 — Mock:** Record yourself and review three separate ways:

1. **Audio only** (eyes closed) — catches filler words, rushed pacing, nervousness in voice.
2. **Video only** (muted) — catches fidgeting, poor eye contact, unfelt tension.
3. **Words only** (read the transcript as if you were the interviewer) — checks if the answer is clear and structured or rambling.

## Good vs bad examples

### Reframing a layoff: same facts, different story

**Scenario:** Candidate laid off from Meta two years ago keeps getting asked "Why would you leave Meta?" and "Why do you want to work here?"

**Bad (original story, Jonathan's verbatim description):**

> So first she said, oh, Meta moves slowly and I love to innovate. So I left to become an independent consultant. And the interviewers were polite but unconvinced, right? Because then the obvious question becomes, well, then why are you applying to another large company?

**Good (revised story, Jonathan's verbatim description):**

> She came clean that she was laid off and clarified that it was not performance related. Then she talked about using that time to build products as a consultant, learning how to go from zero to one. And finally, she explained that she now wanted to bring that experience back into a big company to create impact at scale.

**Why it matters:** The bad version invents a fully voluntary, unforced-error narrative that immediately opens a bigger, harder-to-answer follow-up. The good version tells the truth, closes the "not performance related" risk question explicitly, and reframes the gap as a deliberate skill-building period that makes her *more* valuable to a large company now. As Jonathan summarizes:

> Same facts, completely different story. And that made all the difference.

### The three-phase prep system vs skipping straight to answers

**Scenario:** Candidate wants to walk into an interview feeling calm and confident.

**Bad:** Trying to "pretend to be calm," or jumping straight to rehearsing answers without first researching the dream-candidate profile.

**Good (Jonathan's verbatim framing):**

> Real confidence does not come from pretending to be calm. It comes from knowing deep down that nobody else can help prepare you. So if you want to feel calm and confident like that, then your prep needs to happen in three phases. Research, development, and mock.

**Why it matters:** Confidence is presented as a byproduct of systematic preparation (research → development → mock), not a mindset trick.

---

### Mock practice: reviewing three channels separately

**Scenario:** Candidate has finished writing and rehearsing answers and thinks they're interview-ready.

**Bad:** "Reading answers in your head" and assuming that's sufficient practice.

**Good (Jonathan's verbatim instructions):**

> Record yourself answering interview questions and then review it in those three ways. First, audio only. So close your eyes and just listen. You're gonna hear filler words, rushed pacing and nervousness in your voice immediately. Second, video only. Mute the sound and watch your body language. You're gonna see fidgeting, poor eye contact and tension that you didn't feel. And third is words only. Read the transcript of your recording as if you were the interviewer. Is it clear and structured or rambling?

**Why it matters:** Each channel (sound, sight, words) hides different problems from a candidate reviewing their own performance holistically; isolating them surfaces issues that would otherwise go unnoticed.

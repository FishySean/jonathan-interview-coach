## Core principles

- **The real constraint is never the difficult person — it's your collaboration capacity.** Removing one "Karen" doesn't guarantee future Karens won't show up.
- **You don't control who pours into your cup (your collaboration capacity); you only control how big the cup is.**
- **Conflict at work is normal and unavoidable — others are allowed to disagree with you and even dislike you.** Accepting this is the key to increasing your capacity.
- **Rumination is self-inflicted.** Thoughts like "this is so unfair" are you pouring into your own cup — the difficult colleague isn't paying the cost of the conflict, you are.
- **"Disagree and commit" (Amazon's principle)** applies to working with people you dislike, not just to business decisions.

## Interview philosophy

Not directly about interview evaluation — this Short is workplace conflict-management coaching. The underlying diagnostic move Jonathan models (useful for a coach in any context) is reframing a presenting complaint ("I want to quit because of Karen") by testing whether removing the trigger actually solves the underlying pattern: "Imagine Karen disappears tomorrow. Does that guarantee you that you can avoid future Karens from ever showing up in your career?"

## Evaluation criteria

Signals of low vs. high collaboration capacity:

- **Low capacity:** suffering silently (smiling in public, crying at lunch), seeing only two options (suffer or quit), letting a difficult colleague "live rent free in your head."
- **High capacity:** escalating out-of-scope demands appropriately (borrowing a manager's authority), not internalizing public criticism, recognizing a third option ("navigating through it") beyond suffer-or-quit.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Believing that removing the difficult person (quitting, transferring) permanently solves the problem, rather than addressing your own collaboration capacity.
- Seeing only two options in a difficult relationship — suffer or quit — instead of a third: navigating through it.
- Ruminating on thoughts like "this is so unfair, why is she like this, why do I have to work with someone so difficult" — which Jonathan frames as self-inflicted harm ("that is yourself pouring into your own cup").
- Absorbing out-of-scope demands from someone who isn't even your manager (e.g. doing data entry work that "is not Ann's job") without escalating.
- Letting a colleague's public criticism live in your head instead of "laughing it off."

## Follow-up questions

The diagnostic question Jonathan used to reframe the presenting problem (verbatim):

- "Imagine Karen disappears tomorrow. Does that guarantee you that you can avoid future Karens from ever showing up in your career?"

Follow-up self-check questions implied by the framework:

- "Is this a suffer-or-quit situation, or is there an option C — navigating through it?"
- "Am I pouring into my own cup right now with thoughts like 'this is so unfair'?"
- "Who is actually paying the cost of this conflict — them, or me?"

## Answer frameworks

**The cup framework:** Your collaboration capacity is like a cup. Its size determines how much conflict you can absorb before you overflow (suffer silently or snap). You can't control who pours into it (the workplace isn't kindergarten — you can't just avoid people you dislike), but you can control the cup's size by accepting that conflict and dislike are normal.

**"Disagree and commit" applied to people (not just decisions):**

> There's this famous saying from Amazon. Disagree and commit. To increase your collaboration capacity, you need to accept this fact that conflict is going to happen between coworkers. Others are allowed to disagree with you and even dislike you.

**Tactical moves that grew Ann's capacity (verbatim):**

> When Karen started asking her to work on stuff that wasn't in scope, she escalated to her manager and borrowed his authority. When Karen challenged her publicly and laughed it off, she stopped letting Karen live rent free in her head because she finally realized Karen wasn't paying the cost of their conflict. She was.

## Good vs bad examples

### Suffer-or-quit vs. a third option

**Scenario:** Ann, a data analyst, is ordered around, given out-of-scope data entry work, and publicly criticized by Karen — a stakeholder on another team who isn't even her manager. In public she smiles and moves on; at lunch she cries in her cubicle. She wants to quit.

**Bad (Ann's framing, verbatim):**

> The only option to get out of this living hell is to quit, right?

**Jonathan's reframe (verbatim):**

> Not so fast. I asked Ann a simple question. Imagine Karen disappears tomorrow. Does that guarantee you that you can avoid future Karens from ever showing up in your career?

**Good (the realization, verbatim):**

> You might be able to leave Karen behind right now, but I'm going to be real with you. I've grown my career dealt with some difficult people. And I know sooner or later, Karen always shows up again. And if every time this happens, the only solution you know is to either suffer every day or leave, then the real constraint on your career is not Karen. It's your collaboration capacity.

**Why it matters:** Fixating on removing the person leaves you unprepared for the next difficult colleague; building capacity is the transferable skill.

---

### Who controls the cup

**Scenario:** Ann feels she has no power over a hostile coworker relationship.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Now, you don't get to control who is pouring stuff into your cup. The workplace isn't like kindergarten, right? You can't just say, I don't like playing with you. I want to go play with someone else. The real world is full of people that we cannot avoid. But there is one thing that you can control and that is how big your cup is.

**Bad:** Believing your only lever is controlling other people's behavior (who's on your team, who you have to work with).

**Good:** Focusing effort on growing your own capacity to absorb and navigate conflict, since you can't control who's around you.

---

### Rumination as self-inflicted harm

**Scenario:** Ann silently resents Karen and repeats unfair-feeling thoughts to herself.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Acceptance is the key here because every thought that you have, like, this is so unfair. Why is she like this? Why do I have to work with someone that's so difficult? That is yourself pouring into your own cup and only saw two options in this difficult relationship. Option A was to suffer or option B was to quit.

**Bad:** Rehearsing grievance thoughts, which Jonathan frames as self-harm ("pouring into your own cup"), while seeing only suffer/quit as options.

**Good:** Accepting that conflict and dislike are normal at work, which opens up "option C" — navigating through it — instead of suffering or quitting.

---

### Escalating out-of-scope demands and shrugging off public criticism

**Scenario:** Karen keeps assigning Ann out-of-scope work and challenging her publicly.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> When Karen started asking her to work on stuff that wasn't in scope, she escalated to her manager and borrowed his authority. When Karen challenged her publicly and laughed it off, she stopped letting Karen live rent free in her head because she finally realized Karen wasn't paying the cost of their conflict. She was.

**Bad:** Silently absorbing out-of-scope work and internalizing public criticism (Ann's earlier pattern — smiling in public, crying at lunch).

**Good:** Escalating scope issues to your actual manager (borrowing their authority) and laughing off public challenges instead of ruminating.

**Why it matters:** The person who ruminates pays the cost of the conflict, not the aggressor — so stopping the rumination is itself a form of capacity growth.

## Core principles

- **Polish serves the speaker, not the audience.** The most effective communication isn't impressive — it's understandable.
- **Your "poor English" can be a superpower**, because it forces structured ideas in the simplest words with as few words as possible.
- **If you don't understand someone, that is not your fault. They need to communicate clearly.** Confusion is the speaker's responsibility to resolve, not the listener's failure.
- **There are exactly two ways to bring clarity: ask for it, and provide it.**

## Interview philosophy

This Short is about clarity in day-to-day workplace communication (meetings, stakeholder conversations) rather than interview evaluation specifically, but the same standard applies to how a candidate/employee should be judged: jargon-heavy, "polished" language (e.g. "let's take it offline," "circle back," "double click on the rigor," "noodle on ideas") signals sophistication to the speaker but often creates confusion for the listener. Jonathan frames the person who can *simplify* a polished, complicated message as the one demonstrating real command of the material and leadership — not the one producing the polish.

## Evaluation criteria

What "good" communication looks like in this framework:

1. **Structured ideas** delivered in the simplest words.
2. **As few words as possible.**
3. **Strong logic**, not fancy vocabulary, driving the message.
4. **Willingness to pause and ask for clarity** — read as leadership, not weakness.
5. **Ability to reduce a polished/complicated message to its essence** and restate it back to confirm understanding.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Believing that sounding "professional" requires bigger words and nicer-sounding phrases (e.g. turning "let's talk later" into "let's take it offline, circle back, close the loop").
- Assuming confusion when a colleague talks over your head means something is wrong with you ("Am I just slow?") instead of recognizing it as the speaker's failure to communicate clearly.
- Staying silent when confused instead of asking for clarification — and assuming you're the only one who doesn't understand, when "someone else probably doesn't either... they're just afraid to say it."
- Trying to compete with jargon-heavy colleagues by matching their complexity instead of simplifying.

## Follow-up questions

Questions to ask when someone's message is unclear (verbatim):

- "Can you expand on that?"
- "Could you rephrase that?"

## Answer frameworks

**Providing clarity — confirm, propose, timeline, confirm again:**

1. Restate what you think they're asking for in plain language, and confirm: "It sounds like you're asking for X. Is that right?"
2. Propose specific changes (numbered, concrete).
3. Give a timeline for the changes.
4. Confirm the next checkpoint.

**Full template (near-verbatim, from Jonathan's coached example — adapt the specifics):**

> It sounds like you're asking for more story to explain technical details to a non-technical audience. Is that right? Great. Well, then I propose two changes. First, we'll create a key insight title for each slide. And second, we'll add an executive summary so leaders will know what needs to change from the very first slide. These changes will take me two days. Are you okay with meeting in two days to review it again?

**Asking for clarity — simple phrases (verbatim):**

> Can you expand on that? Or could you rephrase that?

## Good vs bad examples

### Jargon vs. plain language — "HR lady" and the frustrated coachee

**Scenario:** Jonathan's coachee, a data analyst in people analytics, works closely with a colleague ("HR lady") who talks like this.

**Bad (verbatim, the jargon-heavy message that caused confusion):**

> Let's make sure we're seeing the forest from the trees. We want more data storytelling so our SLT can grasp it. Let's noodle on ideas, get alignment and double click on the rigor.

**The coachee's frustration (verbatim):**

> I feel like every time she talks to me, she just sounds so smart, but I don't understand her. Am I just slow?

**Jonathan's reframe (verbatim):**

> Nah, I told her something that I wanna tell you as well. If someone speaks and you don't understand them, that is not your fault. They need to communicate clear.

**Why it matters:** Blaming yourself for not understanding jargon keeps you stuck; recognizing it as the speaker's failure frees you to ask for or provide clarity instead.

---

### Speaking up to ask for clarity

**Scenario:** Someone says something in a meeting that you don't fully understand.

**Bad:** Nodding along and staying silent, assuming you're the only confused one.

**Good (verbatim):**

> When someone says something that you don't understand, pause the conversation and ask them to explain more. Like can you expand on that? Or could you rephrase that? Believe me, if you don't understand, someone else probably doesn't either. And they're just afraid to say it. So speak up because in these moments, speaking up itself demonstrates leadership.

**Why it matters:** Speaking up is reframed from a sign of weakness ("I don't get it") into a leadership signal (you're willing to surface what the room is silently missing).

---

### Providing clarity — simplifying a polished, complicated ask

**Scenario:** Decoding what "HR lady" actually wanted, once the jargon is stripped away.

**Jonathan's translation (verbatim):**

> For example, if we take what HR lady said to my coachee, it's actually so simple, it's even kind of dumb. HR lady just wants there to be more story in the presentation because senior leaders don't want to hear the nitty gritty details.

**Bad:** Trying to match her polished phrasing to sound equally sophisticated back.

**Good — the client's full coached response (verbatim, see Section 8 for the complete script):**

> It sounds like you're asking for more story to explain technical details to a non-technical audience. Is that right? Great. Well, then I propose two changes...

**Why it matters (verbatim):**

> By clarifying the message and communicating simply, you've just saved a ton of back and forth and confusion.

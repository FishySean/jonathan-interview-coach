## Core principles

- **Most offers have at least 10% room for negotiation** — but negotiation is a game with rules you need to know to win.
- **Your greatest leverage is always a competing offer** — and if you're currently employed, your current job counts as one too.
- **Nothing motivates a company faster than the fear of losing you.** A credible competing offer triggers that fear instantly.
- **Interviewing and storytelling skill pays off twice** — in more offers, and in higher-paying offers.
- **"We can't go any higher" is sometimes the truth, not a bluff.** Pushing past the real ceiling can cost you the offer entirely.

## Interview philosophy

Told from the hiring manager's side of the table. When a manager identifies a "superstar" candidate, they will move fast (compress rounds, offer near the top of the salary band) because "great talent doesn't grow on trees." But once the offer is out, the negotiation becomes a separate game: the manager's job shifts to trying to close the candidate quickly, while HR and pay bands act as constraints the manager has to fight internally (with documentation) to exceed.

## Evaluation criteria

What signals a hiring manager reads during negotiation:

1. **Composure under pressure** — not signing immediately when asked to close on the spot.
2. **Credible competing pressure** — mentioning a competing offer or willingness to stay put signals you're not desperate.
3. **Discipline about disclosure** — sharing only the most persuasive detail of a competing offer (e.g., higher base) rather than everything.
4. **Calibration** — knowing when the number on the table is actually the ceiling, versus when there's more room.
5. **Closing signal** — a clear commitment line that shows you're one step away from saying yes.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Signing the offer immediately under pressure instead of taking time to negotiate.
- Not mentioning that your current job is itself a form of competing offer/leverage.
- Oversharing full details of a competing offer or current compensation instead of highlighting only the shiniest, most persuasive part.
- Pushing for more money after being told the company can't go higher — Jonathan notes that if this candidate had asked for more than 15%, he "would have had to pass on her and go to our option B candidate."

## Follow-up questions

Questions a candidate (or their coach) should ask before/during a negotiation:

- "Do I have a competing offer I can point to — including my current job if I'm employed?"
- "What's the single shiniest, most persuasive detail of my other offer or comp that I should highlight, without revealing everything?"
- "Am I close enough to the target number to say the closing line and create urgency?"
- "Is 'we can't go any higher' possibly true here — should I take this deal instead of pushing further?"

## Answer frameworks

Jonathan's three negotiation rules (verbatim):

1. > Your greatest leverage will always be a competing offer. Also, if you're already employed, you're in luck because your current job is considered a competing offer too. Your current job signals, hey, I'm not desperate. If you don't pay me well, I'm just gonna stay here.
2. > You don't need to share the actual competing offer or your existing compensation. The key is to highlight the shiniest part of that comp.
3. > When you're close, say these words. If we can agree on this number, I will sign.

**Closing template (verbatim, keep exact wording):**

> If we can agree on this number, I will sign.

## Good vs bad examples

### Refusing to sign on the spot, under an accelerated process

**Scenario:** A candidate Jonathan believed was also interviewing with Meta and Amazon. He compressed two interview rounds into a single super day and pushed HR to offer near the top of the band, then tried to get her to sign the same day.

**Bad:** Signing immediately when the hiring manager applies pressure to close fast.

**Good (Jonathan's description, verbatim):**

> First, she didn't give in to the pressure. She didn't sign anything. Instead, she told us that she also received a competing offer and she was considering staying put in her current company as well.

**Why it matters (verbatim):**

> Now, I can tell, she knew that nothing motivates a company faster than the fear of losing you. And a competing offer does that instantly.

---

### How much of a competing offer to reveal

**Scenario:** Deciding how much detail to share about a competing offer or current pay.

**Bad:** Disclosing every detail of the competing offer or your full current compensation package.

**Good (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> Second, you don't need to share the actual competing offer or your existing compensation. The key is to highlight the shiniest part of that comp. For example, that savvy candidate didn't share everything about the other offer. She just shared that the salary band was higher, that the base salary, right? And that's what's important to her. Who knows, maybe our total comp was actually higher, but that got us to move on the base salary for her.

**Why it matters:** Selectively surfacing the single most persuasive fact moves the specific lever (e.g., base salary) that the company can actually act on.

---

### Knowing where the real ceiling is

**Scenario:** The candidate ultimately negotiated a 15% increase above the original offer.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Eventually I got her the increase she wanted, right? She signed for 15% more. Honestly, I am so happy for both of us because the truth is that if she had asked for any more than that, I would have had to pass on her and go to our option B candidate.

**His warning (verbatim):**

> Remember, if they tell you we can't go any higher, sometimes that's not a bluff. It might actually be the truth.

**Bad:** Assuming every "we can't go higher" is a negotiating tactic and pushing past it.

**Good:** Recognizing that there is a real ceiling, and stopping at the number that gets you the best deal without losing the offer.

---

### Closing the deal

**Scenario:** Negotiation has converged near a final number and the candidate wants the company to move fast.

**Jonathan's closing line (verbatim):**

> And third, when you're close, say these words. If we can agree on this number, I will sign.

**Why it matters (verbatim):**

> That I will sign line creates urgency. It shows that you're serious just one small step away and they will hustle when they know they're close.

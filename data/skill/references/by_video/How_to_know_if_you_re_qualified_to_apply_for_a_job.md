## Core principles

- **Job descriptions are a wish list, not a checklist.** Hiring managers write intimidating JDs describing an ideal "unicorn" they rarely actually hire.
- **If you match about one-third of the requirements, apply.** That third might be the only part the hiring manager truly cares about.
- **Screening is HR's job, not yours.** Stop rejecting yourself before you even apply.
- **Match either the job title or the industry — not necessarily both.** Changing both at once is much harder than changing one.
- **Bullet order in a JD signals priority**, intentional or not — earlier requirements matter more.

## Interview philosophy

Told from the hiring manager's side: Jonathan admits to writing an "intimidating," unreasonable JD (investment banking/consulting background, 5–7 years, people management, tech exposure) while knowing it described a "unicorn" that "everyone was just chasing." When it came time to actually hire, he picked the best available candidate — someone who matched almost none of the stated requirements — and simply hoped she'd prove him right. This reveals that hiring managers themselves don't expect (or usually get) a candidate who checks every box.

## Evaluation criteria

What actually mattered in Jonathan's own hiring decision, versus what the JD stated:

- **Stated "requirements":** investment banking or management consulting background, 5–7 years experience, people management experience, tech industry exposure.
- **Who was actually hired:** 5 years experience from a big bank — no people management, not ex-tech, not ex-consulting or investment banking.
- **Real signal that mattered:** being the best person available in the pool, not a perfect requirements match.

Practical screening heuristics Jonathan uses when reading a JD:
1. Do you meet roughly one-third of the listed requirements?
2. Does your job title or your industry match (at least one of the two)?
3. Do you meet the requirements listed in the earliest bullets (highest-priority signals)?

## Common candidate mistakes

- **Self-rejecting** before applying because you don't match every line of the JD.
- Treating the JD like a strict checklist instead of a wish list.
- Trying to switch both job title and industry in the same move (e.g., accountant in banking trying to become an FPNA manager in tech) instead of changing only one dimension at a time.
- Ignoring the ordering of bullets in the JD — treating a requirement listed last as equally important as one listed first.

## Follow-up questions

Self-check questions a candidate (or coach) should ask before deciding not to apply:

- "Do I meet about a third of the requirements in this JD?"
- "Does my current job title match this role, or does my industry match — even if not both?"
- "Which requirements are listed first in this JD? Do I meet those?"
- "Am I rejecting myself for a reason that's actually HR's job to screen for, not mine?"

## Answer frameworks

Jonathan's three tips for deciding whether/how to apply (verbatim):

1. > If you meet about one-third of the requirements, then just apply. That one-third might be the only part that the hiring manager actually cares about.
2. > Match either the job title or the industry. For example, if you're an accountant in the banking industry, then you can absolutely apply to be an accountant in the tech industry, or you can try to be a FPNA manager in the same banking industry. But you are going to have a harder time becoming an FPNA manager in the tech industry.
3. > Pay attention to the order of responsibilities and skills in the JD. The earlier bullets are usually the most important ones. Many JD writers list an order of priority, either intentionally or subconsciously.

## Good vs bad examples

### The JD as a wish list, not a checklist

**Scenario:** Jonathan wrote an intimidating JD for a strategy and ops role: investment banking or management consulting background, 5–7 years of experience, people management experience, and tech industry exposure.

**Jonathan's own admission (verbatim):**

> Why do we ask for all this? Honestly, because that's what every strategy and ops role asks for. And I used to joke with my recruiter that everyone was just chasing the same single unicorn.

**Bad (the self-rejecting candidate mindset this teaches against):** Assuming you need to match investment banking/consulting background, years of people management, AND tech exposure to be worth applying.

**Good (who actually got hired, verbatim):**

> Someone with five years of experience from a big bank. No people management, not ex-tech, not ex-consulting or investment banking. And I wish that I could say that I took a chance because I'm a visionary and I knew she would succeed. But to be honest, she was the best person that I could get. And I just hoped that she would prove me right.

**Why it matters (his dating analogy, verbatim):**

> Because the truth is, job descriptions are a wish list, not a checklist. It is just like dating. You can say, I'm like, that doesn't mean that you're actually going to get one, right?

---

### Changing job title AND industry at once vs. changing only one

**Scenario:** An accountant currently working in the banking industry is deciding what roles to target next.

**Good (verbatim):** Applying to be an accountant in the tech industry (same title, new industry) — "you can absolutely apply to be an accountant in the tech industry."

**Also good (verbatim):** Applying to be an FPNA manager in the same banking industry (new title, same industry) — "or you can try to be a FPNA manager in the same banking industry."

**Bad (verbatim):** Trying to become "a FPNA manager in the tech industry" — changing both title and industry simultaneously — "you are going to have a harder time" with that combination.

**Why it matters:** Changing two variables at once (title and industry) compounds the difficulty of the transition; changing one at a time is a more realistic path.

---

### Self-rejection vs. letting HR do the screening

**Scenario:** A candidate sees a demanding JD and considers not applying.

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> This is why I'm saying stop rejecting yourself before you even apply. Screening candidates, that is HR's job, not yours. They're throwing a talent pool party. And your only job is to strip down and just jump into the pool.

**Bad:** Deciding on your own that you're not qualified and never submitting the application.

**Good:** Applying whenever you meet roughly a third of the requirements, and letting the actual screening process (HR/hiring manager) make the call.

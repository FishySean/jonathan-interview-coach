## Core principles

- **This question is not about proving you're capable or wanted** — candidates often miss that and waste the answer defending themselves.
- **Interviewers want to know why you're running toward this job, not why you're running away.** Nothing good comes from a backward-looking complaint session.
- **Honesty + forward pivot wins trust.** Directness (especially on layoffs) reads as confidence; then pivot to why this role is the perfect next step.
- **Never trash your last boss.** The interviewer doesn't know your boss — they wonder if you'll say the same about them someday.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan evaluates "why leaving" as a **directional fit question**. Vague reasons ("not challenged enough"), tiptoeing around bad managers, or five-minute layoff explanations all share the same hidden goal — "trust me, I'm capable" — but that's not what the interviewer is asking.

Interviews are "two people sweet talking back and forth" — not performance theater, but showing you care about the mutual fit.

## Evaluation criteria

Score whether the candidate:

1. **Answers the actual question** — forward motivation toward this role, not escape from the last one
2. **Is direct when appropriate** — layoffs stated plainly, no shame, no over-explanation
3. **Builds trust through honesty + confidence** — especially in layoff scenarios
4. **Shows appreciation before pivot** — voluntary departures start positive, then connect growth goals to this role
5. **Points every word forward** — acknowledges reality without dwelling backward

Red flags: vague dissatisfaction; bad-mouthing former manager; lengthy layoff backstory without pivot; only proving employability.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Giving vague reasons like "I'm not challenged enough."
- Tiptoeing around bad work-life balance or a terrible manager instead of pivoting forward.
- Spending five minutes explaining why they got laid off (over-explaining the past).
- Trying to prove "I'm capable, I'm wanted" instead of "this role is my next step."
- Trashing the last boss when leaving voluntarily — triggers "will they say that about me?"
- Hiding hardship entirely instead of acknowledging it honestly then moving on.

## Follow-up questions

When answers are weak, ask:

- "So why this company and this role specifically — not just any job?"
- "What are you running toward, not away from?"
- "If you were laid off, can you say it in one sentence and tell me why you're excited about us?"
- "What did you grow from at your last company — and what couldn't you do there that you want here?"
- "How does opening [specific company initiative from your research] connect to what you want next?"

## Answer frameworks

**Universal rule:** Answer why you're running **toward** this job.

**Scenario 1 — Laid off:**
1. Say it directly. No shame. ("I was laid off along with…")
2. Move immediately to why this role is the perfect next step.

**Scenario 2 — Left voluntarily:**
1. Start with appreciation (even if you hated it there).
2. Pivot: where you want to go + connect directly to this role.
3. Acknowledge the environment honestly if needed — every word pointed forward.

**Jonathan's meta-frame:** Appreciation → growth gained → superpower unused → connect superpower to this role's need.

## Good vs bad examples

### The hidden mistake: proving capability instead of direction

**Scenario:** "Why did you leave your company?"

**Bad (common patterns Jonathan sees):**
- Vague: "I'm not challenged enough."
- Tiptoeing: bad work-life balance, terrible manager — without forward pivot.
- Over-explaining: spending five minutes on layoff details.

**Jonathan's read (verbatim):**

> Underneath all of these answers, they're trying to prove the same thing. Trust me, I'm capable, I'm wanted, but that is not the point of this question.

> Interviewers don't want to hear why you're running away because nothing good comes from that conversation. They want to know why are you running toward this job?

**Good:** State situation briefly (if needed), then spend the answer on why **this role** is the right next step.

---

### Scenario 1: Laid off — honesty builds trust

**Scenario:** Candidate was laid off.

**Jonathan as director (verbatim):**

> As a director, I've seen layoffs happen to low performers and high performers for all types of reasons. So just say the thing and then move on to the part of the answer that will actually win you points, which is why this role is the perfect next step for you.

**Bad:** Lengthy layoff narrative; shame; defensive over-explanation.

**Good:** Direct statement + confident pivot to this company/role.

**Jonathan's favorite coached answer — opening (verbatim):**

> I was laid off along with nearly everyone else in my department. You might've seen it in the news.

**His interviewer reaction (verbatim):**

> So in my mind, I was like, wow, very honest and she's confident. I trust her.

**Then she pivoted (verbatim):**

> To be honest, the day when the layoff was announced, I was relieved. I love to help launch new things, but over the years, my role shifted away from that. I noticed a company before and I was fascinated at how you've revolutionized the financial industry so quickly, but I didn't apply then because I didn't want to leave my team behind. As soon as I was free to move, this was my first call. I'd love to be part of your planned launches like credit card or mortgage products.

**Why it matters:** Honesty + specificity about **their** launches = running toward, not away.

---

### Scenario 2: Voluntary leave — never trash the boss

**Scenario:** Left voluntarily; possibly toxic environment.

**Jonathan on trash-talk (verbatim):**

> When you trash your last boss, the interviewer isn't thinking, wow, that sounds like a jerk. They don't know your boss. They're thinking, is that what you'll say about me one day?

**Bad:** Venting about micromanaging, abusive manager (even if true).

**Good:** Appreciation → pivot → connect superpower to this role.

**Jonathan's extreme hypothetical (verbatim — "purely hypothetical, of course"):**

> Like even if my boss were a micromanaging, abusive narcissist who swore at me in team meetings, purely hypothetical, of course, I'd say I'm grateful for how I've grown here. I've built resilience and attention to detail by working in a high pressure environment. That said, I didn't have the opportunity to use my real superpower here. I excel at solving tough, ambiguous problems at scale. Like when I opened a new market, a company Z I'm looking to get back to that. And from what I understand about this role, opening new international markets is exactly where you need help.

**Jonathan's close (verbatim):**

> Now the sensor doesn't hide anything. It acknowledges the environment honestly, but every word here is pointed forward, not backward.

### Interviews as mutual care, not trickery

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> Some of you might be like, John, you fool, you just got sweet talked, but at the end of the day, what are interviews? Just two people sweet talking back and forth and not to perform, but just show that we care, right?

## Core principles

- **"How do you use AI?" is a fit question** — assessed on culture fit and skill fit for that company/role.
- **Both extremes fail:** "I never use AI" = rigid/slow to adapt; "I use AI for everything" = don't use your brain.
- **Read the room before answering** — research the company's public stance on AI first.
- **Match company stance in your philosophy, then prove with a domain-specific example.**
- **Outsource process, not thinking** — even pro-AI companies want judgment and verification.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan contrasts **Spotify (pro-AI)** vs **Nintendo (anti-AI / human-first creativity)**. Different stances require different framing of the same underlying competence.

Skill fit varies by profession: ops/PM uses AI for coordination (minutes, comms, plans); marketing uses AI in creative brainstorming/copy/campaigns — but also must know **how the profession doesn't use AI**.

Answer structure: **philosophy aligned to company → concrete example in your domain.**

## Evaluation criteria

Score AI answers on:

1. **Cultural alignment** — Does their AI philosophy match what the company publicly values?
2. **Role-appropriate usage** — Examples fit how their function actually uses (and doesn't use) AI
3. **Judgment** — Double-checking, policy/culture verification, not blind delegation
4. **Avoiding tool laundry lists** — Philosophy + proof, not ChatGPT/Claude/Perplexity name-dropping

Red flags: never uses AI; uses AI for everything; generic tool list with no decision framework.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Answering before researching company AI stance.
- "No, I never use AI" in a pro-AI company.
- "I use AI for everything" — looks like no independent thinking.
- Listing tools without showing judgment or domain application.
- Using AI in ways that violate company culture (e.g. AI-generated creative content at human-first company).

## Follow-up questions

When AI answers are weak, ask:

- "What's your company's stance on AI — and how did you adapt your answer to us?"
- "Walk me through a repetitive task you automate vs a task you keep fully human — why?"
- "How do you verify AI output before shipping?"
- "Give an example in **this role** where AI helped and one where you chose not to use it."

## Answer frameworks

**Pre-interview research:** Search `[Company name] stance on AI`

**Answer structure:**
1. One-sentence philosophy matching company culture (pro vs cautious)
2. Domain-specific example showing application + guardrails

**Pro-AI framing:** Intentional use; automate repetitive tasks; always double-check; deliverables reflect what you actually know.

**Anti-AI / human-first framing:** AI helpful in limited situations; never dependent; verify policy + culture + strategy; use as thought partner where appropriate, not for disallowed outputs.

## Good vs bad examples

### The two wrong extremes

**Scenario:** "How do you use AI at work?"

**Bad — never (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> They don't want to hear, no, I never use AI, because that makes you look rigid and slow to adapt.

**Bad — everything (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> And they don't want to hear, I use AI for everything, because then you look like you don't use your brain.

**Good:** Fit-aligned philosophy + role-specific example.

---

### Culture fit: Spotify vs Nintendo

**Scenario:** Pre-interview research.

**Pro-AI example — Spotify (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> For example, if you search Spotify's stance on AI, you'll find that they claim that their best developers haven't written a line of code for months, thanks to Claude. Very pro AI.

**Anti-AI example — Nintendo (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> But if you search Nintendo's stance on AI, you'll realize that they kind of hate AI, and they take pride in the creativity of human work. So anti AI.

**Bad:** Same AI answer at both companies.

**Good:** Tailor opening philosophy to researched stance.

---

### Skill fit by profession

**Operations / PM (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> For example, in operations and project management, often you'll use AI to coordinate projects, including summarizing meeting minutes, drafting communications, and organizing project plans.

**Marketing (Jonathan, verbatim):**

> On the other hand, in marketing, you might use AI during the creative process to brainstorm ideas, draft creative copy, even plan some marketing campaigns.

**Also required:** Understand how your profession **doesn't** use AI.

---

### Pro-AI company answer — Spotify biz ops example

**Scenario:** Business operations at Spotify.

**Jonathan's full sample (verbatim):**

> I use AI in an intentional way to outsource my process, not my thinking. Tactically, this means identifying repetitive tasks that can be automated and enhanced with AI. At the same time, I know AI has its shortcomings, so I always double check and ensure what I deliver reflects what I actually know. For example, in my current role, I use company approved AI tools like Gemini Enterprise to summarize customer feedback and brainstorm product improvements.

---

### Anti-AI company answer — human-first culture

**Scenario:** Company that rejects AI-generated creative work.

**Jonathan's full sample (verbatim):**

> I think AI is helpful in certain situations, but I'm also conscious of its many limitations. I'm never dependent on AI to produce quality work. Before considering using AI, I verified that it aligns not only with company policy, but also with our culture and strategic priorities. For example, in my current role, I identified that using AI to generate creative content was against our human-first culture, and instead I've leveraged it as a thought partner for brainstorming.

## Core principles

- **Three saveable moments** around rejection risk: unclear next steps with recruiter; lukewarm hiring-manager engagement; post-rejection follow-up.
- **Dig out the real concern and address it before the interview ends** — don't leave and regret it later.
- **Unclear next steps from a recruiter = potential red flag.**
- **If they like you, they'll pitch** the company, role, even themselves as a manager; lack of enthusiasm / lost engagement means figure out why.
- **No doesn't mean never — it means not now.** Keep the door open with a specific follow-up message.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan coaches candidates to treat end-of-interview signals as recoverable, not fatal. Top candidates are in demand — hiring managers who want you sell. When signals are cold or vague, your job is to surface the concern, get permission to share a stronger example (or arm the recruiter to "voice over" your resume), and after a no, stay connected for a future yes.

## Evaluation criteria

Score whether the candidate:

1. **Surfaces concerns before leaving** — Asks what's blocking forward movement
2. **Addresses the concern with a concrete example** — Asks permission to share before ending
3. **Reads engagement** — Notices when HM isn't pitching / loses energy and probes fit concerns
4. **Keeps the door open after rejection** — Gracious, specific, asks to check in later

Red flags: accepting vague "we'll let you know"; ending early without probing; repeating website-level culture questions while ignoring cool interviewer energy; disappearing after a no.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Leaving when next steps are unclear without asking about the real concern.
- Not offering a corrective example when a gap (e.g. leadership) is named.
- Asking generic culture questions that get website answers — then accepting early end.
- Ignoring cool / disengaged interviewer behavior.
- Treating rejection as permanent instead of "not now."

## Follow-up questions

Use (or coach the candidate to use) these probes:

- "Based on what you know, is there a reason the hiring manager wouldn't move forward with me after reviewing my resume?"
- "At this point, do you have any remaining concerns about my fit for this role?"
- "Would that be okay before we end today?" / "Would you like that?" (permission to share a better example)
- After rejection: "Would it be okay if I checked in again in a few months?"

## Answer frameworks

**Moment 1 — Recruiter unclear on next steps:**
1. Flag: unclear next steps = potential red flag
2. Ask what's blocking HM forward movement
3. If a concern is named (e.g. lack of leadership), offer a concrete example + ask permission to share so they can voice over your resume with the HM

**Moment 2 — HM not pitching / loses engagement:**
1. Recognize: liked candidates get pitched company / role / manager
2. Ask remaining concerns about fit
3. Name what might have missed (e.g. earlier stakeholder story) + tie to their stated pain + offer a more specific example

**Moment 3 — Rejection with no prior clear signal:**
1. Reframe: no = not now
2. Send door-opening message: gratitude + disappointment without bitterness + interest in future + ask to reconnect in a few months

## Good vs bad examples

### Moment 1: unclear recruiter close

**Bad signal (verbatim setup):**

> Thanks for interviewing with us. I'll share your resume with the hiring manager, and we'll let you know.

**Why it matters (verbatim):**

> If the recruiter is unclear about next steps after your interview, that is a potential red flag. And if you want to save the interview instead of regretting it afterwards, you need to dig out the real concern and address it before the interview ends.

**Good — candidate probe + repair (verbatim):**

> Based on what you know, is there a reason the hiring manager wouldn't move forward with me after reviewing my resume?

> I see. Thank you for clarifying. If my lack of leadership experience might be a concern, I like to share an example of my leadership with you, just in case you have a chance to voice over my resume with the hiring manager. Would that be okay before we end today?

---

### Moment 2: cool hiring manager / culture Q dead-end

**Bad exchange (verbatim):**

> What's our culture like? Collaborative, inclusive, dynamic? You know, a website says it all. Any other questions or we can also end early.

**Diagnostic (verbatim):**

> Hiring managers know that top candidates are in demand, and if they like you, they'll try to pitch the company, the role, even themselves as a manager. … So if you notice that they aren't enthusiastic about answering your questions, or seem to lose engagement, your goal is to figure out why.

**Good — concern probe + better example offer (verbatim):**

> Just one more question. At this point, do you have any remaining concerns about my fit for this role?

> Yeah, I sense that my earlier story about stakeholder management might not have been exactly what you're looking for. I can see how critical stakeholder management is in this role. Since you mentioned misaligned expectations being a major pain point for your team right now. I'd love to share a more specific example, and I think it will give you more clarity about my approach to stakeholder management. Would you like that?

---

### Moment 3: surprise rejection email

**Bad outcome (verbatim setup):**

> An update about your interview. We were impressed with your interview, and the panel thinks highly of your qualifications. Unfortunately, we've decided to move forward with more qualified candidates.

**Reframe (verbatim):**

> Yes, there was no clear sign of rejection and then a rejection email. But remember, no doesn't mean never. It just means not now. Yes, you can turn a no today into a yes tomorrow if you keep the door open with this message.

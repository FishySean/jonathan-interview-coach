## Core principles

- **A "do you have experience with X?" question is a skills check, not story time.** The interviewer wants a yes/no on whether you meet the bar — not your full career narrative.
- **Concise proof beats a long story.** Starting from the beginning and rambling for five minutes tires and annoys the interviewer.
- **Let the interviewer steer the depth.** Offer a high-level summary, then explicitly invite them to dig into whichever part they care about.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan frames skills-check questions ("Do you have experience with this skill?") as fundamentally different from open-ended behavioral questions: the interviewer already knows what they're listening for (a yes/no on bar-meeting), so the candidate's job is to *prove* it efficiently, not to entertain with a full origin story. Drawn from his experience "hiring and growing teams from zero to over 100 people," the winning move is a **concise, structured project summary** that confirms the requirement, then demonstrates it was met.

## Evaluation criteria

- Does the candidate directly confirm they meet the stated requirement (echoing it back) before diving in?
- Is the example structured (problem → solution → impact) rather than meandering?
- Does the answer end with a quantifiable, verifiable impact?
- Does the candidate manage the conversation's depth instead of dumping everything at once?

## Common candidate mistakes

- Hearing a skills-check question and launching into a long story "from the beginning."
- Rambling until the interviewer becomes "tired and maybe a little annoyed."
- Guessing what the interviewer wants to hear instead of asking / inviting them to direct the follow-up.
- Omitting a quantifiable impact at the end of the example.

## Follow-up questions

- "Which part of this project would you like me to go deeper on?"
- "Is that the kind of GTM experience you were looking for?"
- "What specifically about [the requirement] is most important for this role?"

## Answer frameworks

**Jonathan's 4-step structure for skills-check questions (verbatim):**

> First, you echo the requirement back to them. Then you describe the problem you faced, share how you found a solution and end with a quantifiable impact.

1. **Echo the requirement** — confirm you understood what they're checking for.
2. **Problem** — the specific challenge faced.
3. **Solution** — how you solved it (can be a sub-structure like his 3-step MVP → execute → phased rollout).
4. **Quantifiable impact** — a number that proves the result.

**Bonus closing line (verbatim, reusable in any skills-check answer):**

> That's the high level view. I'm happy to go deeper into any part that you want to explore.

## Good vs bad examples

### Skills-check question: "Do you have experience with go-to-market?"

**Scenario:** Interviewer asks a direct experience/skills-check question.

**Bad (Jonathan's description of most candidates):**

> Most candidates hear this and think, oh, I need to start story. So they start from the beginning. And then five minutes later, the interviewer is tired and maybe a little annoyed.

**Good — full worked example (Jonathan's verbatim answer):**

> Yes. And based on what you shared so far, I can see GTM is critical for this role because you need someone who can roll up their sleeves and execute on ambiguous mandates. Is that right? Great. Well, recently, I led GTM for our latest APAC launch. We were first to market, which means that we had zero playbooks and a lot of ambiguity. So I used a simple three step approach. First, I defined a minimum viable product by leading cross functional stakeholders to align on clear KPIs. Second, I executed quickly by distributing responsibilities. Data science helped us find our target segments, product to find our value proposition, marketing ran pricing strategy and distribution. Third, I launched with a phased rollout strategy to mitigate risk and allow us to iterate. We hit over $1 million in first year revenue and that playbook later became the model for other regional launches.

**Why it matters:** The answer opens by echoing the requirement ("I can see GTM is critical for this role because...Is that right?"), confirms mutual understanding, then delivers problem (zero playbooks/ambiguity) → solution (3-step approach) → quantifiable impact ($1M first-year revenue, became a reusable playbook) — all without a single wasted sentence.

---

### Ending the answer: inviting deeper discussion instead of guessing

**Scenario:** Candidate has just delivered a concise summary and isn't sure whether to keep going.

**Bad:** Continuing to add more detail because you're worried the answer wasn't "enough," or stopping abruptly with no invitation to follow up.

**Good (Jonathan's verbatim power line):**

> That's the high level view. I'm happy to go deeper into any part that you want to explore.

**Why it matters:** This line simultaneously signals conciseness and openness, letting the interviewer — not the candidate — decide what to probe next. As Jonathan puts it:

> No more guessing what they want to hear. Just let them tell you.

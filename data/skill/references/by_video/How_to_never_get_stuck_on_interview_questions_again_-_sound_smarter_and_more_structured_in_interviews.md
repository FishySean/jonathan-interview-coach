## Core principles

- **All interview questions are problem-solving questions** — case, technical, behavioral, anything.
- **You already know how to figure things out at work** even when you don't immediately know the solution; interviews just make you forget that under pressure.
- **Don't freeze on unprepared questions** — run the same problem-solving process you'd use on the job.
- **Structure separates you** from candidates who jump straight into an answer without thinking.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan treats every interview prompt as a work problem, not a quiz. Success comes from externalizing a natural problem-solving sequence (clarify → goal → approach → execute → monitor) so answers sound structured even when the question is new. Mental models (80-20, first principles, systems thinking, etc.) live in the "plan" step — they are tools for approach, not a substitute for clarifying and aligning first.

## Evaluation criteria

Score whether the candidate:

1. **Clarifies and gathers context** — What problem? Why this problem?
2. **Aligns on the goal** — What outcome? What does success look like?
3. **Plans an approach** — Uses mental models / frameworks when helpful
4. **Executes** — Delivers recommendation, key insight, or story
5. **Monitors / iterates** — How they'd check success and optimize over time

Red flags: freezing on unprepared questions; jumping into an answer without clarifying goals or success criteria.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Freezing or panicking when the question wasn't prepared.
- Jumping into the answer without thinking (Jonathan's contrast group).
- Forgetting under interviewer pressure that they already know how to solve problems at work.
- Treating case / technical / behavioral as totally different genres instead of one problem-solving process.

## Follow-up questions

When answers feel stuck or unstructured, ask:

- "What problem are we actually trying to solve — and why this one?"
- "What does success look like here?"
- "What's your approach before you give a recommendation?"
- "How would you monitor whether this worked and adjust?"
- "Can you clarify the interviewer's goal before answering?"

## Answer frameworks

**Problem-solving sequence for any interview question:**

1. **Clarify and gather context** — What problem are you trying to solve? Why this problem?
2. **Align on the goal** — What outcome? What does success look like?
3. **Plan your approach** — Mental models / proven frameworks (80-20, first principles, systems thinking, whatever fits)
4. **Execute** — Recommendation, key insight, or story that achieves the goal
5. **Monitor success** — Find areas to improve and optimize over time

**Case-style skeleton (Jonathan's placeholders):**
> I'd like to start by understanding your goals. What does improvement mean specifically here? … So you want to [clarified goal], right? My approach is [mental model]. Based on what I know, I see options A or B. I would recommend B because of XYZ. I would roll it out as a pilot… observe… and adjust from there.

**People / conflict skeleton:**
> Confirm why the skill matters for the role → discard approaches that don't fit the context → focus on the problem, not the person → understand their goals and incentives → work backward to a shared solution → check in regularly.

## Good vs bad examples

### Same mindset: case question ("how would you improve this process?")

**Scenario:** Unprepared case / process-improvement question.

**Bad:** Freeze, panic, or jump straight into a solution without clarifying "improvement."

**Good — Jonathan's structured demo (verbatim):**

> Well, I'd like to start by understanding your goals. What does improvement mean specifically here? I see. So you want to reduce manual work without increasing costs, right? Well, my approach for automation is to use systems thinking, meaning I start from the big picture. Based on what I know about this process, I see two options, A or B. I would recommend B because of XYZ. I would roll it out as a pilot at first, observe if it actually reduces manual labor, and adjust from there.

**Why it works (verbatim):**

> See how that answer comes across in a structured and well-thought-out way?

---

### Same mindset: people problem ("how do you handle conflicts with co-workers?")

**Scenario:** Behavioral / interpersonal question under the same framework.

**Bad:** Jump into a generic "I compromise / I communicate" answer without checking role context.

**Good — Jonathan's structured demo (verbatim):**

> Oh, it sounds like conflict management is critical in this role, since I'm going to have to hold teams accountable, is that right? Okay, so that means that traditional approaches of compromising might not really work here. Yeah, so my approach to conflict, where I'm in a leadership role, is to always focus on the problem, not the person. I would start by understanding their goals and incentives, and then I would work backward from that perspective to ensure that I design a solution that truly works for us both. I'd make sure to check in regularly, to ensure that we're on the same page and that they feel aligned.

---

### Pressure vs natural problem-solving

**Jonathan's reframe (verbatim):**

> None of this is new to you, but when an interviewer puts pressure on you on the spot, you forget that you naturally know how to solve problems, right?

**Core idea (verbatim):**

> All interview questions are problem-solving questions.

> So when you get asked questions that you haven't prepared for, there's no need to freeze or panic. Like, what do you do when you face a problem at work? You might not immediately know how to solve it, but you know how to figure it out, right?

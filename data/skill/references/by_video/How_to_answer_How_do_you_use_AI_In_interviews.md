## Core principles

- **Don't answer "what can AI do?" — answer "how do you decide your working model with AI?"**
- **AI thinking is about stakes, not tool literacy** — "how much AI you know" is the wrong bar.
- **One decision question:** "If this goes wrong, how bad is it?"
- **Low stakes → autopilot** (delegate freely, move fast). **High stakes → copilot/manual** (your judgment leads; never outsource thinking).
- **If you're still doing low-risk tasks manually, you're falling behind.**
- **Job interviews are high stakes — you in the room, not ChatGPT.**

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan sees candidates demo AI ability with **tool laundry lists** (ChatGPT drafting, Claude automation, Perplexity research) — which misses what interviewers actually assess: **judgment about when to delegate vs when to own the thinking**.

High-stakes examples: difficult stakeholder conversation (your judgment leads), leadership presentation (your thinking fully), job interview (you, not ChatGPT).

## Evaluation criteria

Score AI answers on:

1. **Philosophy first** — Clear stakes-based framework before examples
2. **Low-stakes automation** — Evidence they delegate appropriately on low-risk work
3. **High-stakes ownership** — Knows when AI is collaborative at most, not autopilot
4. **Productivity awareness** — Understands cost of **not** automating low-stakes tasks
5. **No tool laundry list** — Unless tied to the working model

Red flags: only listing tools; no stake assessment; using AI for everything including high-stakes judgment calls.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Demonstrating AI with a laundry list of tools and use cases.
- Answering "what can AI do?" instead of "how do you decide when to use it?"
- Still doing meeting summaries, market research, slide outlines manually without reason.
- Using or implying AI assistance in high-stakes moments (interviews, leadership trust moments).
- No contrast between autopilot and copilot modes.

## Follow-up questions

When AI answers are weak, ask:

- "Before you use AI on a task, what's the one question you ask yourself?"
- "Give me a low-stakes task you fully delegate — and a high-stakes one you don't."
- "If this goes wrong, how bad is it — walk me through your decision."
- "Why is manually doing meeting summaries a problem in 2026?"

## Answer frameworks

**Decision question:** If this goes wrong, how bad is it?

**Low stakes → autopilot:** Meeting summary, market research, slide outline — full AI; monitor; move fast.

**High stakes → copilot/manual:** Stakeholder conflict, exec presentation, job interview — your judgment/thinking leads; AI only where necessary; never outsource thinking.

**Interview answer structure:**
1. Philosophy (stakes-based)
2. Low-stakes example from your role (full delegate + why)
3. High-stakes example from your role (autopilot → manual shift)

### Tool laundry list vs real question

**Scenario:** "How do you show AI ability?"

**Bad (Jonathan hears, verbatim):**

> Like, I use Chai GPT for drafting, Claude for agential automation, Perplexity for research.

**Jonathan's reframe (verbatim):**

> Now when I heard that, I realized that most candidates are answering the wrong question about AI. They answer, what can AI do? But the real question is, how do you decide your working model with AI?

**Good:** Share philosophy, then prove with low/high stakes examples.

### The stakes question

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> If you're like me, it comes down to a simple question. If this goes wrong, how bad is it, right?

### Low stakes — autopilot

**Jonathan's examples (verbatim):**

> If the task is low stakes, the mistakes don't matter much. Choose autopilot, delegate freely, move fast.

> Like if you wanna generate a meeting summary, full AI. Market research, full AI. Build a slide outline, full AI.

**Audit prompt (verbatim):**

> Look at your whole work week and ask yourself, what here is low risk? That is where AI should already be running on autopilot.

**Consequence of not automating (verbatim):**

> Because if you're still doing these things manually, then you are already falling behind.

**Bad:** Manually doing all low-risk admin work and bragging about it.

**Good:** Full delegation on low-risk tasks with monitoring.

---

### High stakes — copilot / manual

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> But if the task is high stakes, you have to engage copilot mode.

> There are these moments where if you get it wrong, then you don't fully get it back, right?

**Examples (verbatim):**

> A difficult conversation with a stakeholder, your judgment leads.

> A presentation where you're asking leadership to trust your call, that is your thinking fully.

> Or a job interview, that is you in the room, not chat GPT.

**Bad:** Full AI on leadership trust moments or interviews.

**Good:** Collaborative AI only where necessary; thinking stays yours.

---

### Interview delivery

**Jonathan (verbatim):**

> So when an interviewer asks you to demonstrate your AI approach or thinking, don't list your tools. Share your philosophy, then prove it.

**Closing distinction (verbatim):**

> That is difference between someone who uses AI and someone who knows how to think with it.

## Jonathan's sample answers (verbatim)

### Word-for-word interview answer

- **Central question this Short addresses:** How do you show AI ability in interviews?
- **Interview question(s) this sample answers:** How do you use AI? / How do you approach AI at work?
- **Jonathan's sample answer (verbatim):**

> I believe that AI thinking is not about how much AI you know. It's about what's at stake. I asked myself one question before involving AI. If this goes wrong, how bad is it?

## Core principles

- **If you keep failing and don't know why, you need feedback more than more prep.** Pass/fail alone is outcome, not coaching signal.
- **Real feedback is hard to get by design.** Hiring managers are busy, still hiring, and often trained not to give specific feedback (lawsuit risk).
- **Ask at three moments:** (1) right after the interview, (2) right after you pass to the next round, (3) after rejection.
- **Embed asks in emails you already send** (thank-you, recruiter reply, rejection reply) — lowers friction.
- **HR/recruiters are allies, not opponents.** If they like you, they'll tip you for the next round.
- **Rejection is the best improvement window** — you already know a gap exists; appeal personally: improve-only intent + respect for their insight.

## Interview philosophy

Jonathan as hiring manager explains why bland rejections ("you were great, but we went with a more qualified candidate") are the default. Candidates who learn faster break through defenses by asking at high-impression moments, treating recruiters as partners, and framing feedback as growth — not argument or appeal of the decision.

## Evaluation criteria

Not a candidate-scoring rubric. Closest coaching bar: does the job seeker collect **actionable** signal (how to improve / what to prepare next) rather than only pass/fail outcomes?

Useful self-check:
1. Did you ask while impression was fresh (post-interview thank-you)?
2. After advancing, did you ask recruiter for last-round feedback + next-round tips?
3. After rejection, did you ask with improve-only framing?

## Common candidate mistakes

- Treating pass/fail as the only feedback needed (like "promoted or fired" being the only workplace feedback).
- Skipping thank-you notes / never embedding a feedback ask.
- Not realizing you can ask the recruiter for help between rounds.
- Deleting the rejection email and never asking — wasting the clearest signal that a gap exists.
- Asking in a way that sounds like arguing the decision rather than wanting to improve.

## Follow-up questions

Coach questions for candidates stuck in a fail loop:

- "What specific feedback have you collected in the last three interviews — not just pass/fail?"
- "Did you add a feedback line to your thank-you email?"
- "After you advanced, what did you ask the recruiter about the last round and the next?"
- "After the last rejection, did you send an improve-only ask?"

## Answer frameworks

**Moment 1 — Right after the interview** (impression strongest; shows interest). Add to thank-you email:

> I'm always looking to get better. If you have any feedback on my interview or how I can improve, I would love to learn from it.

**Moment 2 — Right after passing / moving to next round.** Reply to HR with two questions:

> Did the last interviewer share any feedback about her last round? Anything I should keep in mind?

> And do you have any tips about what I should expect in the next round? Anything I should definitely prepare?

**Moment 3 — After rejection.** Add this line (appeals to personal connection; improve-only + respect):

> I understand you're busy, but I'd be genuinely grateful for even the simplest feedback you have. My only goal here is to improve. And after crossing paths with you on my journey, I respect your insight.

**Reframe:** Pass/fail = outcome. Feedback = how to influence the next outcome.

## Good vs bad examples

### Prep spiral vs feedback gap

**Scenario:** Candidate keeps failing interviews without knowing why.

**Jonathan's diagnosis (verbatim):**

> If you keep failing interviews and you don't know why, you don't need more preparation. You need more feedback. But of course, the problem is getting real feedback, right? Not, you were great, but we decided to go with a more qualified candidate.

**Why managers withhold (verbatim):**

> I know as a hiring manager why it's so hard to give real feedback. I have a busy job to do. I'm still focused on hiring the right person. And I've probably been trained to not give you specific feedback in order to avoid discrimination lawsuits.

---

### Pass/fail is not feedback

**Scenario:** Candidate thinks outcome notification is enough.

**Bad belief (paraphrased by Jonathan, then rebutted):**

> why do I need feedback? Won't they just let me know if I pass or got rejected? Isn't that the feedback?

**Verdict (verbatim):**

> That's a mistake. That's like saying the only feedback I need at work is either getting promoted or getting fired, right? Like passing or failing an interview is the outcome. And if you want to influence that outcome, then you need to know how you can improve.

**Good:** Embed feedback ask in the thank-you you should already send (line in Answer frameworks).

---

### After advancing: recruiters as allies

**Scenario:** HR says you're moving to the next round.

**Advantage (verbatim):**

> asking the right questions here can give you a critical advantage because most other candidates don't even know that they can ask the recruiter for help.

**Ally reframe (verbatim):**

> Remember, HR recruiters are your allies in your job search, not your opponents. Their goal is to fill this position with a qualified candidate. So if they like you, they're gonna go out of their way to give you some valuable tips.

**Good:** Ask the two questions in Answer frameworks.

---

### After rejection: delete vs ask

**Scenario:** Rejection email lands.

**Bad impulse (verbatim):**

> I know this hurts. You wanna delete that rejection email forever and go eat a whole tub of Ben and Jerry's.

**Good reframe (verbatim):**

> But just wait. A rejection is actually the best opportunity to improve your interview skills because you know that a gap exists, right?

**Why the improve-only line works (verbatim):**

> The goal of this line is to appeal in a personal way to your interviewer. You've already met some of them and built some kind of connection, right? So this line appeals to that emotional connection by clarifying that you only want the feedback to improve, not for other reasons. And you respect their opinion.

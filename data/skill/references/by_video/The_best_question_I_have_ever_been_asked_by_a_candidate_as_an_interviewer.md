## Core principles

- **~90% of candidate questions are generic obligations** ("supposed to ask") — a waste of everyone's time.
- **Best candidate question forces the interviewer to define 1-year success** and imagine hiring you.
- **Good questions create mutual upside** — not just "get the job," but a good decision for both sides.
- **Listen and follow up** on what the interviewer truly needs; that conversation is the win.
- **Visualization is powerful** — making the HM picture you in the role changes the interview dynamic.

## Interview philosophy

Speaking as an interviewer, Jonathan wants fewer performative Q&As and more questions that extract what success actually looks like beyond the JD. The standout question made him define year-one success he hadn't articulated, revealed the candidate cared about mutual fit, and opened a real dialogue (even running over time).

## Evaluation criteria

Score whether the candidate's closing questions:

1. **Avoid generic culture filler** — Not "what's your culture like?"
2. **Force concrete success criteria** — What must be true in one year for the hire to be "right"?
3. **Signal mutual decision quality** — Care about the HM's outcome, not only getting hired
4. **Follow up on the real need** — Listen, then probe for a concrete example of the stated need

Red flags: obligatory generic questions; no follow-up after the HM opens up; only self-serving logistics questions.

## Common candidate mistakes

- Asking culture / "dynamic and fast-paced and inclusive" questions they don't care about.
- Feeling they must ask *something* even when they have no real question.
- Stopping after one clever question without listening or following up.
- Focusing only on getting the job, not on whether hiring them would be a good decision for the manager.

## Follow-up questions

When coaching "questions for me," push toward:

- "If I get the job and we look back in one year, what would I have to accomplish to make you say hiring me was the right decision?"
- After they answer: "Is there an example of [the need they named] where you've wanted [the capability they described]?"
- "What does success look like one year from now — specifically, beyond the job description?"

## Answer frameworks

**Standout closing question (template — preserve wording):**
> If I get the job and we look back in one year, what would I have to accomplish to make you say hiring me was the right decision?

**Why it works (Jonathan's reasons):**
1. Makes the interviewer **imagine hiring** the person (visualization)
2. Challenges them to **summarize and share what they truly want**
3. Shows the candidate wants it to be a **good decision for both**
4. Creates a **real conversation afterward**

**Follow-up pattern after HM answers:**
1. Affirm alignment with what they said
2. State how you fit that need
3. Ask for a concrete example of that need in their world

## Good vs bad examples

### Generic "any questions?" theater

**Bad — obligatory culture Q (verbatim dynamic):**

> Have any questions for me? … Truth is, like 90% of candidates just ask a generic question that they don't really have because they feel like they're supposed to, right? Like, what's your culture like? Oh, our culture is dynamic and fast-paced and inclusive. It's a waste of everyone's time, right?

**Jonathan's private wish (verbatim):**

> Have any questions for me? Please say no. Sometimes, as an interviewer, I really want to add that last part.

---

### The best question + what it unlocked

**Good question (verbatim):**

> If I get the job and we look back in one year, what would I have to accomplish to make you say hiring me was the right decision?

**Interviewer reaction (verbatim):**

> I once had a candidate ask me that, and I was automatically like, wow, I like that. Really good question. And then I thought hard because honestly, I hadn't defined that to myself. Sure, I'd written the job description and I knew what problems I had, but specifically, what does success look like one year from now?

**Why it was good (verbatim):**

> It was good because it made me imagine hiring this person, and visualization is powerful, right? It was good because it challenged me to summarize and share what I truly wanted. It was good because it showed me that this person didn't just care about getting the job like everyone else. She wanted it to be a good decision for both of us. And it was so good because of the conversation that we had afterwards.

---

### HM's real need + candidate follow-up

**Jonathan's answer as interviewer (verbatim):**

> So after thinking for a while, I was like, you know, all the job description skills aside, what I really need is someone who I can trust to solve ambiguous problems. I need you to be this Swiss army knife that I can deploy across very different projects.

**Candidate follow-up (verbatim — as Jonathan recounts):**

> I'm so glad to hear that. I know I'm the type of person who doesn't just tolerate ambiguity. I thrive in it. Is there an example of an ambiguous project where you've wanted the Swiss army knife?

**Outcome (verbatim):**

> And I love that follow up question. And before I knew it, we were 10 minutes over time.

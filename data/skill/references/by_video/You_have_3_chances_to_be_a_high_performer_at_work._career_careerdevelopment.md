## Core principles

Three truths Jonathan wished he’d heard earlier (from the manager/performance-review side):

1. **Performance ≠ how hard you work.** Effort is an input; only **outputs that impact the business** matter.
2. **Soft skills multiply hard skills.** Senior leaders live in meetings — communicate, align, decide; hard skills plateau while soft skills must grow exponentially.
3. **You must be political to succeed.** Politics = unwritten rules of influence, relationships, communication — lean in or complain and stall.
- Hard work got you here; **it won’t get you there.**

## Interview philosophy

(Workplace high-performer / promotion lens — transferable to behavioral interviews.) Visible grind and spreadsheets lose to people who ask how the business makes money and cut busywork. Directors hire future managers who are easy to work with, not only technical brilliance. Solo night/weekend projects without sponsors get killed as “not priorities.”

## Evaluation criteria

- Business-impact questions vs hour/slide counting
- Clarity + collaboration vs isolated genius
- Sponsorship / buy-in / decision-maker relationships vs lone-wolf brilliance

## Common candidate mistakes

- Last one in office / endless spreadsheets as “top performer” identity.
- Absorbing low-value busywork dumped by people who understand the game.
- Believing only more technical skill → growth.
- Avoiding “toxic” politics while waiting for good work to be noticed.
- Building side projects without aligning to company priorities or finding a champion.

## Follow-up questions

High-performer self-checks:

- “How does this business make money?”
- “How does my work connect?”
- “How do I do more of that and less busy work?”
- “Am I still avoiding politics, or learning to use it to grow?”

Director lens: who becomes future manager — A (brilliant but unclear/hard to work with) or B (clear, collaborative, liked)?

| # | Truth | Practice |
|---|-------|----------|

| 2 | Soft skills multiply | Explain value; collaborate; be easy for leaders to work with |

## Good vs bad examples

### Effort vs impact coworker

**Bad path:** Collect hours worked and slides built.

**Good coworker:** Leaves 5pm, one project last quarter — but asks money/connect/more-impact questions; dumps busywork on people who don’t understand the game.

### Engineer A vs B

**A:** Technically brilliant; can’t explain in meetings; hard to work with; avoided by teammates/manager.

**B:** Weaker technically; clear ideas; expands impact via collaboration; liked by teammates/stakeholders/leaders.

**Director (90% people/leadership):** Picks B as future manager.

### Politics / sponsorship

**Bad:** Nights/weekends building solo projects expecting celebration → boss: drop it, we have priorities (“stop messing around”).

**Shift:** Problem wasn’t the boss — no influence, no sponsor, ideas not connected to goals. After buy-in + decision-maker relationships → everything changed.

## Jonathan's sample answers (verbatim)

### Three truths

- **Central question this Short addresses:** Three chances / truths to be a high performer at work.
- **Interview question(s) this sample answers:** Career growth / performance / leadership potential (behavioral transferable).
- **Jonathan's sample answer (verbatim):**

> First, performance has nothing to do with how hard you work. Effort is just an input, and inputs don't matter. The only thing that matters is the outputs that you produce to impact the business.

> Like, how does this business make money? How does my work connect? And how do I do more of that and less busy work? Then they turn around and drop that low value busy work onto people who don't understand the game. Don't let them drop it on you.

> Second, soft skills are a multiplier for hard skills. … Because the harder you go, the more you have to grow exponentially in soft skills, not hard skills.

> Third, you must be political to succeed. … politics is simply the unwritten rules of how influence, relationships, and communication work in the workplace. And you can either accept that and lean into it, or you can keep complaining and getting nowhere.

> Once I learned to communicate my ideas to get buy-in and build relationships with decision makers, everything changed. So ask yourself, are you still avoiding politics, or are you learning to use it to grow your career? Because hard work got you here, but it won't get you there.
